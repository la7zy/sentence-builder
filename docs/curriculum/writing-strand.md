# Writing strand — sentence → paragraph → essay → research paper (+ free writing studio)

Prepared 2026-09-05 for the ATTP English platform. This plan fills the writing row of the contract in
`docs/curriculum/00-platform-frame.md` (lesson loop, exercise catalogue, bank and test rules) and follows the
five bands and the text-types ladder in `docs/standards-mapping.md` (Section C, row 13).

## 0. How to read this plan

- **Bands (student-facing names only):** Foundations (A1), Building (A2), Connecting (B1), Expanding (B2), Mastery (C1). No school-grade labels anywhere in the app.
- **Levels of the strand:** 1 Sentence · 2 Paragraph · 3 Essay · 4 Research paper · 5 Free writing studio. A learner works through the levels that exist at their band (see §1); certificates are per level × band.
- **Lesson IDs:** `W<level>-<band letter>-<nn>`, e.g. `W2-C-04` = writing level 2 (paragraph), Connecting, lesson 4. Band letters F, B, C, E, M.
- **Each lesson row gives:** title · rule/skill in one plain sentence · what must be covered (variants) · 1-2 examples · common ESL errors · exercise plan. Exercise plans use only these catalogue names: `mc, click, next, gap, order, error-spot, transform, match, classify, sequence-paragraphs, write` (8-20 items per lesson, hints before answers, feedback that says *why*).
- **The lesson loop is the frame's loop:** explain (one rule per screen, read-aloud) → exercises → level test (15 questions drawn at random from a 100-120 item bank, pass 80% = 12/15, plus a writing part) → certificate → remediation (every bank item and every writing-check is tagged with the lesson that teaches it).
- **Existing material is placed, not duplicated:** the Sentence Builder course (7 units, 37 lessons, 2-word sentences → 18-word complex scrambles; types mc/click/next/gap/order) is the spine of Level 1 at Foundations–Connecting (§2.0); the Sandwich Essay guide is the spine of the sandwich paragraph (W2-C-01) and the five-paragraph essay set (W3-C-07..09); the Comparative Essay guide is the spine of the comparative essay set (W3-E-08..10).
- **Topics:** adult and real (work, family, health, money, housing, community, travel) and general knowledge (history, inventions, discoveries, famous places and people). Never school life as the learner's own life.
- **Where sources disagree** the plan teaches the standard American classroom convention, names the variant, and answer keys accept both where both are correct (frame §6). Disagreements and choices are listed in §7.

### 2.1 Foundations — sentence lessons (W1-F)

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W1-F-01 | What makes a sentence | A sentence has a subject and a verb, starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop. | subject = who/what; verb = does/is; capital + end mark; one idea per sentence; *I* always capital | *My sister works in a bank.* / *The bus is late.* | dropping *be* (*She teacher.*); dropping the subject (*Is cold today.*); no capital; comma instead of full stop | click 4 (find subject/verb) · mc 3 · next 3 · order 4 · error-spot 3 = 17 |
| W1-F-02 | Three kinds of sentence | A statement tells, a question asks, a command gives an instruction, and each ends with its own mark. | full stop / question mark / exclamation (rare); question word order (*Do you…? Is she…?*); commands start with the verb (*Open the window.*); *please* | *You live here. / Do you live here? / Live here!* | statement order in questions (*You live here?* in writing); *?* after statements with *if*; double marks *?!* | classify 5 · mc 3 · transform 4 (statement→question/command) · gap 3 = 15 |
| W1-F-03 | Two ideas, one sentence: and / but | Join two short sentences with *and* (adds) or *but* (surprises), and do not repeat the subject if it is the same. | compound subject/verb (*Ana and Luis work.* *I cook and clean.*); two full sentences with *, and* / *, but*; *or* for choice; no sentence starting *And* yet | *I work at night, but I sleep well.* / *He speaks Spanish and Arabic.* | *and* chains (*and… and… and*); comma before every *and*; *but* + *however* together | order 4 · transform 4 (join two sentences) · mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 2 = 16 |
| W1-F-04 | Not a sentence yet (fragments 1) | A group of words without a verb or without a subject is not a sentence; add the missing part. | missing verb (*My brother in Canada.*); missing subject (*Is very cold.*); missing *be* before adjective/noun; a list is not a sentence | *My brother lives in Canada.* / *It is very cold.* | *Is* sentences without *it*; *-ing* form as the only verb (*She working.*); *Because tired.* | classify 5 (sentence / not yet) · error-spot 4 · gap 4 · transform 3 = 16 |
| W1-F-05 | Starting differently (variety 1) | Begin some sentences with a time or place phrase, followed by a comma, so that not every sentence starts with *I*. | openers: *Every day, / On Mondays, / At work, / In my country,*; comma after the opener; keep subject + verb after it | *In the morning, I take the bus.* / *At home, we speak Somali.* | opener without a comma; opener with no subject after (*In the morning take the bus.*); *Every day I am go.* | order 4 · transform 4 (move the opener) · next 3 · mc 2 · write 1 (5 sentences about your day, 2 with openers) = 14 |

### 2.2 Building — sentence lessons (W1-B)

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W1-B-01 | Simple and compound sentences | A compound sentence joins two complete sentences with a comma and *and, but, or, so* (also *yet, for, nor*). | independent clause; FANBOYS; comma before the conjunction when both sides are full sentences; no comma for a compound verb; *so* = result, *or* = choice | *The shop was closed, so we went home.* / *She called and left a message.* | comma splice (*It rained, we stayed in.*); *so* + *because* together; comma before *and* in a compound verb | classify 5 (simple/compound) · click 3 (find the two clauses) · transform 4 · error-spot 3 = 15 |
| W1-B-02 | First complex sentences | A dependent clause (*because, when, if, before, after, although*) needs a main clause; put a comma only when the dependent clause comes first. | subordinator meanings; two orders (*Because X, Y. / Y because X.*); comma rule; no comma before *because* normally | *When I arrived, the meeting had started.* / *I stayed home because I was sick.* | *Because* clause standing alone; comma before *because*; *although… but* in one sentence; *when* + *will* (*When I will arrive*) | transform 5 (join with a given subordinator) · order 4 · mc 3 · gap 3 = 15 |
| W1-B-03 | Fragments 2: the dependent-clause fragment | A clause that starts with *because/when/if/although* cannot stand alone; attach it or cut the word. | recognising the fragment after a full sentence; two fixes (join with the sentence before/after; delete the subordinator); *-ing* phrase fragments; *For example* fragments | *I was late. Because the train stopped.* → *I was late because the train stopped.* | treating *Because…* answers as sentences; fixing by adding *it is*; over-joining into run-ons | error-spot 5 · classify 4 · transform 5 = 14 |
| W1-B-04 | Run-ons and comma splices | Two complete sentences cannot sit together with nothing or only a comma between them; use a full stop, a comma + conjunction, or a subordinator. | fused sentence; comma splice; three fixes; *then/also/however* are not conjunctions (*, then* = splice); when to keep two short sentences | *I finished work, I went to the gym.* → *I finished work, and then I went to the gym.* / *After I finished work, I went to the gym.* | *, then*; *, however,*; very long *and* strings; fixing with a comma only | error-spot 5 · mc 3 · transform 5 (fix three ways) · classify 3 = 16 |
| W1-B-05 | Adding detail | Grow a bare sentence with adjectives, adverbs and prepositional phrases that answer *which, how, where, when*. | adjective before noun; adverb of manner after verb; prepositional phrases of place/time/movement at the end or front; not more than one of each at first | *The man waited.* → *The old man waited quietly at the station for an hour.* | adjective after noun (*a car red*); adverb between verb and object (*I speak well English*); *in* vs *at* vs *on* | transform 5 (expand with a given detail) · order 4 · gap 3 · click 2 · write 1 (expand 4 bare sentences) = 15 |
| W1-B-06 | Sentence variety 2 | Mix short and longer sentences and vary the first word so the paragraph does not sound like a list. | four openers (time, place, dependent clause, adverb); combine two shorts into one compound/complex; keep one short sentence for effect; avoid three sentences in a row starting with the same word | *I have a car. I drive to work. I park far away.* → *I have a car, so I drive to work. Because parking is expensive, I park far away.* | every sentence starting with *I*/*The*; joining everything into one endless sentence; losing the subject after an opener | order 3 · transform 5 (combine/re-open) · mc 3 · sequence-paragraphs 2 · write 1 (rewrite a 6-sentence list paragraph) = 14 |

### 2.3 Connecting — sentence lessons (W1-C)

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W1-C-01 | Subordinators for reason, time, contrast, condition, purpose | Choose the subordinator that shows the exact relationship (*although, while, since, unless, so that, even though, as soon as, whereas*). | families: cause (because/since/as), time (when/while/until/as soon as), contrast (although/even though/whereas), condition (if/unless), purpose (so that); comma rule; *while* = time or contrast | *Although the salary is low, the hours are flexible.* / *Unless we book early, the tickets will sell out.* | *even* alone (*Even it rained*); *despite* + clause (*despite it rained*); *so that* vs *so*; *unless* + *not* | match 4 (subordinator↔meaning) · gap 5 · transform 4 · error-spot 3 = 16 |
| W1-C-02 | Combining with relative clauses | Put a second sentence about a noun inside the first with *who, which, that, where, whose*. | defining clause, no commas; *who* people / *which* things / *that* both; *where* places; *whose*; no extra pronoun (*the man who he…*); *that* cannot follow a comma | *The nurse who treated my father speaks Farsi.* / *This is the app which I use for my budget.* | resumptive pronoun (*the flat that I rent it*); *which* for people; *what* instead of *which/that*; dropping the relative pronoun as subject | transform 5 (combine two sentences) · click 3 · error-spot 4 · mc 3 = 15 |
| W1-C-03 | Semicolons and conjunctive adverbs | Two related complete sentences can be joined with a semicolon, or with a semicolon + *however/therefore/for example* + comma. | `; ` between independent clauses only; `; however,`; the comma-splice trap with *however*; full stop is always also correct; colon is not a semicolon | *The flat is small; however, the rent is fair.* / *We left early; the roads were icy.* | *, however,* between two sentences (splice); semicolon before a fragment; capital after semicolon | error-spot 5 · transform 4 (join three ways) · classify 3 · mc 3 = 15 |
| W1-C-04 | Parallel structure 1 | Items in a list or pair must have the same grammatical form (noun with noun, *-ing* with *-ing*, clause with clause). | lists with commas + *and*; pairs with *and/or/but*; comparisons with *than/as*; verb forms in lists; fix by matching the first item | *She likes cooking, reading and walking.* (not *…and to walk*) / *The job needs patience, accuracy and a calm voice.* | mixed *-ing*/*to*; article dropped on later items (*a pen, book and ruler*); unequal clauses after *not only… but also* | error-spot 5 · transform 5 · classify 3 · mc 2 = 15 |
| W1-C-05 | Sentence combining, rhythm and openers (variety 3) | Vary length and structure deliberately: a long sentence to build, a short one to land. | opener types: adverb (*Suddenly,*), prepositional phrase, dependent clause, *-ing* phrase (subject must match), appositive preview; combining 3 short sentences 2 ways; reading aloud to hear rhythm | *Prices rose. People stopped buying. Shops closed.* → *When prices rose, people stopped buying, and within a year the shops had closed. Nobody was surprised.* | dangling *-ing* opener (*Walking home, the rain started.*); over-long sentences (>35 words); same opener repeated | transform 5 · order 3 · sequence-paragraphs 2 · error-spot 3 · write 1 (rewrite an 8-sentence choppy paragraph) = 14 |
| W1-C-06 | Sentence editing workshop | Read each sentence alone, check it is complete, correctly joined, and consistent in tense and agreement, then fix it. | checklist: complete? joined correctly? tense consistent? subject-verb agreement across an intervening phrase? pronoun clear?; one fix per problem; reading backwards sentence by sentence | *The manager, as well as the staff, are…* → *is…*; *He said he will come* → *would come* (reported) | fixing one error and creating another; tense shifts mid-sentence; agreement with the nearest noun | error-spot 6 · transform 5 · classify 3 · mc 2 = 16 |

### 2.4 Expanding — sentence lessons (W1-E)

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W1-E-01 | Compound-complex sentences | A sentence can have two main clauses and at least one dependent clause; punctuate each join by its own rule. | structure map (MC + MC + DC in any order); commas: after a fronted DC, before the coordinator; keeping subjects visible; when to split | *Although rents have risen, wages have not kept pace, so many families now share flats.* | double conjunction (*Although…, but…*); losing the second main clause's subject; five-clause monsters | click 4 (label clauses) · classify 3 · transform 4 (build from three sentences) · error-spot 3 · mc 2 = 16 |
| W1-E-02 | Parallel structure 2 | Correlative pairs (*not only… but also, either… or, both… and*) and parallel clauses must be balanced word for word. | placement of the correlative directly before each parallel part; parallel headings and bullet lists; parallel comparisons (*more X than Y*); parallel clauses across a sentence and across a paragraph | *The plan is not only cheaper but also faster.* (not *…not only is cheaper but also fast*) | misplaced *not only*; mixing noun and clause after *either*; *both… as well as* | error-spot 5 · transform 5 · order 3 · mc 3 = 16 |
| W1-E-03 | Appositives and participial phrases | Reduce a whole clause to a noun phrase (appositive) or an *-ing/-ed* phrase to say more with fewer words. | appositive = renaming noun phrase set off by commas; restrictive vs non-restrictive; present vs past participle phrases; phrase must attach to its noun | *Marie Curie, a Polish-born physicist, won two Nobel Prizes.* / *Built in 1889, the tower was meant to be temporary.* | wrong participle (*the boring student* vs *bored*); commas missing round the appositive; participial phrase attached to the wrong noun | transform 6 (reduce a clause) · click 3 · error-spot 4 · mc 2 = 15 |
| W1-E-04 | Misplaced and dangling modifiers | Put a describing phrase next to the word it describes, and make sure that word is actually in the sentence. | dangling *-ing/-ed* openers; misplaced *only/almost/just*; squinting adverbs; two fixes (move the phrase; give the phrase its subject) | *Driving to work, the traffic was terrible.* → *Driving to work, I hit terrible traffic.* / *She almost paid $300* vs *She paid almost $300.* | dangling openers (very frequent in ESL writing); *only* placed early; relative clause far from its noun | error-spot 6 · transform 5 · mc 3 = 14 |
| W1-E-05 | Concision | Cut words that add nothing: empty openers, redundant pairs, stacked nouns, needless *which/that* clauses. | *there is/it is* openers; redundancies (*past history, end result*); wordy phrases (*due to the fact that* → *because*); prefer verbs to nominalised nouns when the actor matters; target 10-20% shorter | *There are many people who believe that…* → *Many people believe that…* | translating long native-language phrasing; deleting needed articles when cutting; cutting hedges that carry meaning | transform 6 (cut to a word limit) · error-spot 4 · mc 3 · classify 2 = 15 |
| W1-E-06 | Punctuating clauses: semicolon, colon, dash, parentheses | Semicolons separate equals, colons introduce, dashes interrupt for emphasis, parentheses tuck away an aside. | colon after a complete sentence only; dash pairs vs single dash; parentheses and end marks; semicolons in a complex list; comma + coordinator vs semicolon choices | *She had one goal: a stable job.* / *The flat — the cheapest we saw — had no heating.* | colon after *are/include*; dash instead of every comma; capital after colon; hyphen used as dash | classify 4 · error-spot 5 · gap 4 · transform 3 = 16 |
| W1-E-07 | Emphasis and sentence shape (variety 4) | Control where the weight falls: end-focus, cumulative vs periodic sentences, cleft sentences. | end position carries the new/important idea; periodic sentence delays the main clause; cleft *What matters is… / It was the cost that…*; short sentence after long ones; avoid emphasis by capitals/exclamation | *It was not the price but the delivery time that lost the contract.* | overusing *very/really* for emphasis; exclamation marks in formal text; putting the key idea in a subordinate clause | transform 5 · order 3 · mc 3 · sequence-paragraphs 2 · write 1 (rewrite 6 flat sentences for emphasis) = 14 |

### 2.5 Mastery — sentence lessons (W1-M)

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W1-M-01 | Verbal and absolute phrases | Gerund, infinitive, participle and absolute phrases condense clauses and create variety when their subject relationship is clear. | gerund phrase as subject/object; infinitive of purpose; participial phrases front/mid/end; absolute phrase (*noun + participle*: *The meeting over, we left.*); punctuation | *Having reviewed the data, the board postponed the launch.* / *Its budget cut in half, the clinic closed two wards.* | dangling participles; gerund vs infinitive after verbs (*suggest going*); absolute phrase written as a fragment | transform 6 · click 3 · error-spot 4 · classify 2 = 15 |

## 3. Level 2 — Paragraph

### 3.1 Foundations — paragraph lessons (W2-F)

### 3.2 Building — paragraph lessons (W2-B)

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W2-B-01 | The topic sentence | The first sentence names the topic and says one thing about it that the rest of the paragraph will show. | topic + what about it; not a question, not a fact list, not too big (*Food is important*) or too small (*I ate rice*); position: first (standard convention; variants named in §7) | *My grandmother's kitchen was the busiest room in the house.* (topic: the kitchen; idea: busiest) | announcing (*I will write about…*); a title instead of a sentence; a topic sentence that promises two topics | mc 4 (pick the best topic sentence) · classify 4 (too broad/too narrow/good) · error-spot 3 · transform 3 (fix the announcing sentence) · write 1 (3 topic sentences from 3 topics) = 15 |

### 3.3 Connecting — paragraph lessons (W2-C)

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| W2-C-01 | The sandwich paragraph (point, evidence, explanation, link) | A body paragraph makes a point, gives something real as evidence, explains why it proves the point, and links back or forward. *(Sandwich Essay guide, stages 1-4.)* | arguable point vs fact; evidence = example, number, event, expert; explanation answers "so what?"; link sentence; 4-8 sentences; the part-time-work example from the guide | *Point: A part-time job teaches money skills. | W2-C-05 | The process paragraph | Explain how to do something in ordered steps, with the materials first, imperatives or present tense, and warnings where needed. | topic sentence naming the process and why; materials; steps with sequence words; imperative vs *you*; warnings (*Be careful not to…*); closing result | *Registering with a doctor takes four steps. | W2-C-06 | The opinion paragraph | State an opinion, support it with two or three reasons each with a short explanation, and close with the opinion restated. | opinion verbs (*I believe, In my view*); reason signals (*First, Second, Finally*); explanation after each reason; concession sentence optional (*Some people say…, but…*) | *In my view, adults should keep a monthly budget. | W2-C-07 | The compare/contrast paragraph | Compare two things on the same points using *while/whereas/however/similarly*, in point-by-point order. *(Feeds Comparative Essay guide, stages 2-3.)* | same-category check; 2-3 points; point-by-point in one paragraph; comparison language (*both, similarly, unlike, whereas, in contrast, more … than*); a judgement sentence at the end | *Buses and trains both serve my route, but they differ in cost, speed and comfort. …* | *than* vs *then*; *the same like*; *compare with/to*; comparing on different points for each item | match 4 · gap 4 · transform 3 (join with whereas/while) · mc 2 · write 1 (compare two options, 100-150 words) = 14 |
| W2-C-08 | Descriptive and narrative paragraphs 2 | Show rather than tell with specific nouns, strong verbs and sensory detail; in narrative, use dialogue punctuation and past perfect for earlier events. | *show, don't tell*; sensory detail; specific verbs (*trudged* not *walked slowly*); quotation marks and commas in dialogue; past perfect for the earlier past; pacing (slow down at the key moment) | *"We're closing," the guard said, and he had already locked the side door.* | tell-only (*It was very beautiful*); quotation punctuation (*She said "…".*); past perfect everywhere; overused adjectives | error-spot 4 (dialogue punctuation) · transform 4 (tell → show) · gap 3 · mc 2 · write 1 (a moment that changed a plan, 120-150 words) = 14 |

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

## 4. Level 3 — Essay

Essay level starts at Connecting (the "short, simple essay" of CEFR B1). Essay *types* each get a lesson set; the five-paragraph/sandwich essay is taught at Connecting as scaffolding and explicitly loosened at Expanding and Mastery (§7, disagreement 1).

### 4.1 Connecting — essay lessons (W3-C)

| ID | Title | Rule / skill | Must cover | Examples | Common ESL errors | Exercises |

## 5. Level 4 — Research paper

Research skills start procedurally at Building (a giving-credit list, folded into the Building paragraph test), are *introduced* at Connecting (MLA/APA in-text + simple list), *applied* through a sourced paper at Expanding (MLA 9 and APA 7 in depth), and reach full style-manual conformity at Mastery (Chicago notes-bibliography and author-date added, formatting, presenting). Style versions taught: MLA Handbook 9th ed. (2021), APA Publication Manual 7th ed. (2020), Chicago Manual of Style 18th ed. (2024; differences from the 17th named in W4-M-04/06).

## 6. Level 5 — Free writing studio

### 6.1 How the studio works

- Open at every band, no lessons and no bank; it is practice with feedback. A student picks a prompt (or writes their own, tagged with a text type), chooses the text type, and gets the word-count target. A finished piece earns a **Studio badge** (per band: 10 pieces that pass the machine checks); the teacher may grade any piece with the §6.5 rubric ("teacher-graded" mark).
- Every submission runs (1) the rules engine (Harper) for spelling, grammar and punctuation flags, with each flag explained and linked to the lesson that teaches it; (2) the structure checks for the chosen text type (§6.3); (3) optional on-device coaching tips (never a grade). The student may revise and resubmit without limit; the history is kept.
- Hard checks (H) must pass for the piece to count; soft checks (S) only flag, because they are heuristics the teacher confirms. Thresholds per band: engine flags per 100 words ≤ 8 / 6 / 4 / 3 / 2 (Foundations → Mastery); longest sentence ≤ 20 / 30 / 35 / 40 / 40 words.

### 6.3 Structure checks per text type (machine-checkable rubric items)

All checks operate on paragraphs (split on blank lines or indents), sentences (rules-engine splitter) and word lists kept in the app (transition families, opinion phrases, hedges, boosters, concession/turn signals, sequence words, comparison words, cause-effect connectors, conclusion signals, reporting verbs, citation regexes for MLA/APA/Chicago). "Keyword" = a content word from the prompt or from the student's own thesis sentence.

| Text type | Hard checks (must pass) | Soft checks (flag for the student; teacher confirms) |
|---|---|---|
| Sentences (Foundations) | ≥ 5 sentences; every sentence starts with a capital and ends with . ? or !; each has a finite verb; ≥ 1 sentence with *and/but*; no sentence over 20 words | three sentences in a row with the same first word |
| Paragraph — general | one paragraph block; word count in band range; first sentence is a statement of ≥ 6 words containing a keyword and not *I will write / This paragraph*; ≥ 3 supporting sentences; ≥ 1 transition or order word | last sentence introduces a new number or proper noun (new detail); a sentence with no keyword overlap with the topic sentence or its neighbours (unity) |
| Paragraph — descriptive | ≥ 4 adjectives; ≥ 2 prepositional phrases of place; tense consistent (≥ 80% finite verbs in one tense group) | *very/really/nice* more than twice; no sensory noun |
| Paragraph — narrative | ≥ 70% finite verbs past tense; ≥ 2 time signposts; quotations (if any) correctly punctuated | no past perfect where *before/after* + past appears; *suddenly* > 1 |
| Paragraph — process | ≥ 3 sequence words; ≥ 2 imperatives or *you + verb*; steps ≥ 3 sentences | mixed *you/we/one* |
| Paragraph — opinion | opinion phrase in sentence 1 or 2; ≥ 2 reason signals; final sentence restates (keyword overlap with sentence 1) | no explanation sentence after a reason (no *because/this means/for example*) |
| Paragraph — compare/contrast | both items named in the first sentence; ≥ 2 comparison words; ≥ 2 points (two sentences containing both items or a *while/whereas* clause) | *than/then* confusion; *the same like* |
| Sandwich paragraph | point (statement); ≥ 1 evidence marker (number, name, *for example*, *according to*); ≥ 1 explanation verb; link sentence with a keyword from the point | evidence sentence with no explanation sentence following |
| Essay — general (all types) | ≥ 3 paragraphs (Connecting), 4-6 (Expanding), 5-8 (Mastery); a thesis sentence in the first paragraph (statement ≥ 8 words, keyword, not an announcement); each body paragraph starts with a topic sentence (statement with a thesis keyword or a signpost); ≥ 1 paragraph-level signpost and ≥ 1 sentence transition; conclusion paragraph with a conclusion signal or thesis keyword and no new number/proper noun; no sentence over the band limit; word count in range; Expanding+: no contractions, no *you* | thesis is a question; body paragraphs differ in length by more than 3:1; the same signpost twice; *In this essay I will* |
| Five-paragraph / sandwich essay | exactly 5 paragraphs; three body paragraphs each passing the sandwich-paragraph checks; thesis previews three items (three keywords reappear as topic-sentence keywords) | a concession signal appears in a body paragraph (guide's convention; flagged if absent) |
| Opinion / for-and-against | opinion: position phrase in the intro and the conclusion; ≥ 2 reason signals across body topic sentences. For-and-against: one body paragraph with ≥ 2 "for" signals and one with ≥ 2 contrast signals; position phrase only in the conclusion | opinion phrases in the "against" paragraph |
| Argumentative | all essay checks; ≥ 1 concession signal followed by a turn signal (*however/yet/nevertheless/but*) within the same or the next sentence group; ≥ 2 evidence markers across the body; Expanding+: ≥ 2 in-text citations when sources are supplied | absolute words (*always, everyone, never*) > 2; a fallacy pattern (*since X happened, Y*) |
| Comparative | both items in the thesis; comparison words in ≥ 3 body sentences; point-by-point: each body topic sentence names a criterion (noun from the thesis) and both items appear in that paragraph; block: a final weighing paragraph with both items | one item mentioned more than twice as often as the other |
| Cause and effect | ≥ 3 cause-effect connectors from ≥ 2 families (preposition, conjunction, verb); thesis contains *cause/effect/reason/result/led/impact* or a keyword from the prompt | *because of* + clause; *affect/effect* confusion; hedge missing before a causal claim with no evidence marker |
| Problem and solution | a problem section with ≥ 1 evidence marker before any solution signal (*one solution / could / should*); ≥ 1 solution paragraph with a mechanism verb (*works by, involves, requires*) and ≥ 1 limitation signal (*however, cost, risk, but*); conclusion with a recommendation modal (*should/could/must*) | solution section longer than the problem section by more than 3:1 |
| Summary and response | a reporting verb + source name in the first two sentences; summary paragraph(s) with no opinion phrase; response paragraph(s) with ≥ 1 response move signal (*agree, disagree, however, extends, overlooks*); ≥ 1 in-text citation; overlap with the source < 8 consecutive words outside quotation marks | summary in past tense; summary longer than 40% of the essay |
| Descriptive / narrative essays | the paragraph checks of the type applied per paragraph; ≥ 3 paragraphs; narrative: an orientation paragraph (who/where/when nouns) and a reflection paragraph (present-tense finite verbs in the last paragraph) | dialogue with no punctuation errors but no information (teacher) |
| Process / how-to essay | ≥ 3 paragraphs of steps; sequence words in each; imperatives or *you*; materials/purpose sentence in the intro; result sentence in the conclusion | mixed person |
| Synthesis / policy / critical review (Mastery) | ≥ 3 distinct sources cited in text; every in-text author present in the list; no body paragraph citing only one source (synthesis); policy: criteria nouns listed in the intro and each reappearing in the body; review: claim/evidence/assumption signals (*claims, evidence, assumes, omits*); ≥ 2 hedges, ≤ 2 boosters | by-source paragraphing; verdict without criteria |
| Research paper | word count; ≥ 4 (Expanding) / ≥ 6 (Mastery) distinct in-text authors; list ↔ in-text match; single-style regex; block-quote length rule; overlap check; heading parallelism (if headings); NB: footnote numbers sequential | intro longer than 20% of the paper; a section with no citation; a source cited only once (possible padding) |

### 7.2 Where sources disagree, and what the plan teaches

1. **The five-paragraph essay.** Writing centres and composition scholars (Dartmouth; Inside Higher Ed 2016; ASCD; *Bad Ideas About Writing*) call the form formulaic and unlike real published prose; defenders call it a sound first scaffold; Purdue OWL keeps teaching the classic modes while noting the criticism. *Taught:* the sandwich/five-paragraph essay is the scaffold at Connecting (guide-based, timed); from Expanding the number of body paragraphs follows the argument and the rubric rewards structure that "serves the argument (not only the formula)"; at Mastery a mechanical five-paragraph shape on a complex prompt scores 2 on Organisation.
2. **Topic sentence first.** Purdue OWL and most ESL coursebooks put it first; UNC and Harvard say it is usual but not obligatory. *Taught:* first at Foundations–Connecting (hard check), free position from Expanding (soft check).
3. **Thesis at the end of the introduction.** The American classroom convention (Purdue, Harvard) vs traditions that state the position later or in the conclusion (some exam and UK models). *Taught:* thesis anywhere in the first paragraph (the check accepts any position in paragraph 1); for-and-against essays state the own view only in the conclusion, by design.
4. **First person in academic writing.** APA permits *I/we* for the author's own actions; MLA is neutral; many instructors forbid it. *Taught:* natural in opinion and narrative writing; sparing in argumentative essays; in research papers only if the assignment allows.
5. **Where the counterargument goes.** Harvard lists several positions (after the introduction, before the conclusion, within paragraphs). *Taught:* all three, one required.
6. **Serial (Oxford) comma.** Required by MLA, APA and Chicago; commonly omitted in British usage (British Council models). *Taught:* use it; accepted either way in free writing; bank items never hinge on it.
7. **Page numbers for paraphrases.** MLA requires a location when the source has one; APA "encourages" but does not require; Chicago includes it. *Taught:* give the page for a paraphrase whenever the source has pages, in every style.
8. **Purdue OWL vs the style manuals.** OWL lags at edition changes (its Chicago pages moved to the 18th edition only in 2025) and simplifies. *Taught:* the manual is the authority; OWL explains; where they differ the manual's form is the key and OWL's older form is listed as a named variant only if it was correct in a still-common earlier edition.
9. **Chicago 17 vs 18.** The 18th drops the place of publication, lists up to six authors (then three + *et al.*), repeats names instead of the 3-em dash, italicises website names that are publications, and lets a month/season be dropped from journal citations. *Taught:* 18th; 17th forms accepted in keys only for assignments that specify the 17th.
10. **MLA 8 vs 9 personal interview.** MLA 8: *Lopez, Maria. Personal interview. 3 Mar. 2026.*; MLA 9: *Lopez, Maria. Interview. Conducted by …, 3 Mar. 2026.* *Taught:* MLA 9; MLA 8 accepted.
11. **APA 6 vs 7.** "Retrieved from", publisher location, running head on student papers and *et al.* only from the second citation are all APA 6. *Taught:* APA 7 only; APA 6 forms are marked wrong with an explanation, because students meet them in old guides.
12. **Register of "informal" tasks.** British Council A1-B1 tasks (messages, forum posts, emails) welcome contractions and *you*; academic essays do not. *Taught:* register follows the text type; the no-contraction/no-*you* checks apply only to essays and papers at Expanding and above.
13. **"In conclusion."** Some centres call it mechanical. *Taught:* fine at Connecting; soft-flagged at Expanding+ with alternatives.
14. **CRAAP vs newer frameworks.** Lateral reading (SIFT) is now often preferred to checklist evaluation. *Taught:* CRAAP as the checklist at Connecting, lateral reading added at Expanding, methodology and conflict-of-interest checks at Mastery.
