# Curriculum plan: grammar sections 8-12

Prepared 2026-09-05 for the ATTP English Platform. Fills the contract in `00-platform-frame.md` (lesson loop, exercise catalogue, bank and test rules, checkpoints) and follows the spiral in `standards-mapping.md` Section C rows 8-12 (standards tags in brackets refer to that file). Covers: **8 Prepositions & adverbials · 9 Conjunctions & clause combining · 10 Questions, negatives & reported speech · 11 Punctuation & capitalisation (with spelling and commonly confused words) · 12 Cohesion & text connectives.** All five sections are introduced at Foundations and revisited at every band, so each section has five band units (25 units in all).

## 0. Conventions used in this plan

**Bands.** Foundations (A1) · Building (A2) · Connecting (B1) · Expanding (B2) · Mastery (C1). No school-grade labels anywhere in student-facing text. Topics are adult only: work, family, health, money, community, travel, general knowledge (history, inventions, discoveries).

**Lesson entry format.** Code `S.Band.n` (S = section, Band = F/B/C/E/M). Each lesson gives: title · the rule in one plain sentence · the variants and exceptions that must be covered (cross-checked against the sources in the section's registry) · example sentences · common ESL errors with first-language tags where the contrastive literature (Swan & Smith, *Learner English*) documents them · the exercise plan. Exercise counts use only the twelve catalogue types allowed for this strand: **mc, click, next, gap, order, error-spot, transform, match, classify, listen-choose, dictation, say-it**. Every lesson has 8-20 items (frame §4).

**How the types are used here.** `next` = the student picks the word that must come next (tests word order and collocation, e.g. *look forward* → *to*). `order` = words into a sentence; in Section 12 only, the units to be ordered may be whole sentences of a 3-5 sentence text (still the `order` type). `transform` covers join, split, report, reorder, punctuate and register rewrites; keys list every accepted variant. `say-it` items are practice only and are never placed in banks or tests (speech scoring is not an answer key). `dictation` may be tested (key + tolerant matching).

**Section test, every unit.** 15 questions drawn at random from the unit bank; pass = 12/15 (80%); a new draw on every attempt. Standard test mixes by band (the bank keeps the same proportions ×7 and every item is tagged with the lesson that teaches it):

| Band | Test mix (15) | Bank size rule |
|---|---|---|
| Foundations | mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · match 1 · order 1 · error-spot 1 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1 | 100-120; equal share per lesson |
| Building | mc 3 · gap 4 · order 1 · error-spot 2 · transform 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 | same |
| Connecting | mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · order 1 · listen-choose 1 | same |
| Expanding | mc 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 4 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1 | same |
| Mastery | mc 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 1 · match 1 · dictation 1 | same |

**Remediation rule (all units; numbers vary by band).** Each missed item names its lesson. Fail → the student is sent to every missed lesson: explain screens replay (read-aloud on), then a fresh exercise set from that lesson's unused pool (Foundations 8 items, Building 10, Connecting/Expanding/Mastery 12); a lesson missed twice in one attempt replays in full. Then a new 15-question draw that excludes every item seen in the student's last three attempts. Three consecutive fails flag the student on the teacher dashboard and lock the retake until the teacher reopens it. Passing issues the unit certificate (student name, date, verification code).

**House style where sources disagree** (frame §6: teach the standard American classroom convention, name the variant, accept both in keys where both are correct): serial comma taught, omission accepted when the list is unambiguous · commas and periods inside closing quotation marks (US), British "logical" placement accepted only when the student's spelling is consistently British · *data is* accepted in general writing, *data are* required only in the APA/scientific register lessons · *different from* taught; *different than* (US) and *different to* (UK) accepted · singular possessive *Jones's* taught, *Jones'* accepted · lowercase after a colon taught unless the colon introduces two or more sentences or a quotation; the APA rule (capitalise a complete sentence after a colon) accepted · American spelling taught; British accepted when consistent throughout an answer · closed em dash taught; spaced en dash accepted · terminal prepositions, sentence-initial *however/and/but*, *hopefully* as a sentence adverb, *who* for *whom* in informal register and *less* with counts of money/time/distance are all taught as standard (Merriam-Webster), with the "rule" that forbids them named as a myth learners will meet.

**Certificate naming.** "[Section name] — [Band] Certificate", e.g. "Prepositions & Adverbials — Foundations Certificate".

## Section 8 — Prepositions & adverbials

Spiral: F in/on/at place & time [CI-A1] → B movement; prepositional phrases for detail [CI-A2; CA PII.6.5.Em] → C adverb clauses; phrases and their effect on agreement [CA PII.6.5.Br; TEKS 7.10.D.iv] → E phrases set off by commas/dashes; arranging phrases for style [TEKS E1.9.D.v; VA24 8.LU.1.D] → M compounding prepositional phrases to condense [CA PII.11-12.7.Br].

### 8 · Foundations (A1) — 4 lessons, 60 exercises, bank 104

- **8.F.1 Where is it? in, on, at (place).** Rule: *in* means inside a space, *on* means on a surface, *at* means at a point or a place where you do something.
  - Must cover: in a room/box/city/country; on a table/wall/floor/shelf; on Main Street (US; *in Main Street* UK, accepted); at home/work/school/the door/the bus stop/12 Oak Street; in bed; in the hospital (US) vs in hospital (UK, accepted); on the bus/train/plane vs in the car/taxi; in the corner of a room vs on/at the corner of a street; in the picture (never *on*); no article in *at home, at work*.
  - Examples: "The keys are on the table." / "My son is at school." / "We live in Houston." ESL errors: preposition dropped ("I live Houston" — Chinese, Russian, Arabic); one preposition for everything (Spanish/Portuguese *en*, Arabic *fi* → *in*); "on the picture/photo" (Russian, Polish, French *sur*); "at Houston" (Arabic, Korean, Hindi/Urdu).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · match 2 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.F.2 When? in, on, at (time).** Rule: *at* goes with a clock time, *on* with a day or date, *in* with a month, year, season or part of the day; *at night* is the exception.
  - Must cover: at 7:00/noon/midnight/night/lunchtime; on Monday/May 5/my birthday/Monday morning/the weekend (US; *at the weekend* UK, accepted); in June/2025/summer/the morning/the 1990s; **no preposition** before last/next/this/every/yesterday/today/tomorrow ("next Monday", not "on next Monday"); at Christmas (the season) vs on Christmas Day; at the moment; in ten minutes (= from now).
  - Examples: "The bank opens at 9:00 on weekdays." / "She was born in 1980." / "I visit my parents on Sundays." ESL errors: "in Monday" (Spanish, Portuguese, Russian, Chinese); "in the night" (Spanish, French, German); "on next week" (most L1s); "at the morning" (Arabic, Chinese).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · match 2 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.F.3 Next to, between, in front of, behind, under, above, near, across from.** Rule: these words say where one thing is compared with another thing.
  - Must cover: next to = beside; between two things (among a group, receptive only); in front of vs opposite/across from (US) ; behind; under/below vs over/above; near (*near to* UK, accepted) vs far from; inside/outside; *here/there* go after the verb ("Put it there"); no "of" after behind/near.
  - Examples: "The pharmacy is between the bank and the post office." / "Wait in front of the clinic." / "The bus stop is across from the library." ESL errors: "behind of" (Spanish *detrás de*); "in front of" used for *opposite*; "beside" vs "besides"; "near of/near to" (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · match 2 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.F.4 Where time and place words go in a sentence.** Rule: at the end of a sentence put the place before the time, or move the time to the front.
  - Must cover: "I work at the hospital at night" (place then time); front position "On Monday I start at 8" — comma after a fronted phrase is optional when it is short (Chicago 6.31; OWL) and taught as required from four words up (8.B.5); adverbs of frequency before the main verb, after *be* (link Section 7); *every day, yesterday, tomorrow* at the start or the end; never between the verb and its object.
  - Examples: "We eat lunch at work at noon." / "Tomorrow I have a doctor's appointment at 3." / "She is never late." ESL errors: time before place ("I go at 8 to work" — German, Dutch); adverb between verb and object ("I like very much coffee" — Spanish, Italian, French, Arabic); sentence-final *always* (Chinese).
  - Exercises (15): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 2 · order 4 · match 1 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Foundations mix; bank 104 (26 per lesson; type proportions of the test ×7; every listen-choose item has studio audio); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (8 fresh items per missed lesson; new draw excludes the last three attempts). **Certificate:** Prepositions & Adverbials — Foundations Certificate.

### 8 · Building (A2) — 5 lessons, 80 exercises, bank 110

- **8.B.1 Movement: to, into, out of, onto, off, across, along, through, over, past, toward, from...to.** Rule: movement words show where something goes; *to* shows the place you reach.
  - Must cover: go to work/school/the bank but go **home** (no *to*); arrive **in** a city/country, arrive **at** a building/event, never *arrive to*; get to; into/out of vs in/out; onto/off; through (inside) vs across (surface); toward (US) / towards (UK, accepted); from Monday to Friday (US also *Monday through Friday*); return to (not *return back to*); enter a room (no *into*; *enter into an agreement* is a fixed idiom).
  - Examples: "Walk across the street and go into the pharmacy." / "The train from Chicago arrives at the station at 6." / "She drove through the tunnel and over the bridge." ESL errors: "arrive to" (Spanish *llegar a*, French, Italian, Arabic); "go to home" (most L1s); "enter into the room" (Hindi/Urdu, Chinese); "return back" (Arabic, Hindi/Urdu, Chinese).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.B.2 Time periods: for, since, during, until/till, by, ago, before/after, from...to.** Rule: *for* says how long, *since* says when it started, *by* means no later than, *until* means up to that time.
  - Must cover: for + length vs since + starting point; *ago* after the period ("two years ago"); during + noun vs while + clause (link 9.B); until/till (*till* informal); by (deadline) vs until (continuing); in + period = at the end of it ("in ten minutes"); in time vs on time; at the end vs in the end.
  - Examples: "I have worked here for three years." / "Please send the form by Friday." / "The shop is closed during lunch." ESL errors: "since two years" (French *depuis*, German *seit*, Spanish *desde hace*, Italian *da*); "during two hours" (Spanish *durante*); *until* for *by* ("I need it until Monday" — German *bis*); "before two days" for *two days ago* (Arabic, Hindi/Urdu).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.B.3 Phrases that add detail: with, without, about, of, for, by, like, as.** Rule: a preposition plus a noun adds one detail — who with, what about, how, or why — and sits right after the word it describes.
  - Must cover: with/without (company, instrument); by + transport/means (by bus, by email) but *on foot, in my car*; about (topic); of (part/belonging) vs *'s* (link Section 2); for (purpose, recipient); like (similar) vs as (job/role: "works as a nurse"); made of/from; a cup of coffee; "the woman with the red bag" (phrase after its noun).
  - Examples: "He came to the meeting with his manager." / "She works as a cashier at a store near the airport." / "We talked about the new schedule for an hour." ESL errors: "go with bus" (Spanish, Portuguese, Turkish); "work like a nurse" (Spanish/Portuguese *como*, French *comme*); "married with" (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French); "discuss about" (Hindi/Urdu, Arabic, Chinese); "explain me" (Spanish, French — needs *to me*).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.B.4 Verb + preposition and adjective + preposition.** Rule: some verbs and adjectives always take one particular preposition — learn the pair as one word.
  - Must cover: the 24 highest-frequency pairs (listen to, look at/for/after, wait for, depend on, think about/of, ask for, pay for, belong to, apologize for/to, agree with, good at, afraid of, interested in, tired of, famous for, married to, similar to, proud of); verbs with **no** preposition (enter, discuss, answer, reach, marry, tell, call, phone); -ing after the preposition ("interested in learning"); *different from* taught, *different than* (US) / *different to* (UK) accepted (Merriam-Webster: all standard).
  - Examples: "It depends on the weather." / "I'm interested in nursing." / "Please wait for me at the entrance." ESL errors: "depend of" (Spanish, French, Portuguese, Italian); "listen the radio", "wait me" (Spanish); "good in" (Hindi/Urdu, Chinese, Arabic); "answer to the question"; "call to him/phone to him" (Spanish *llamar a*, Italian).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 5 · error-spot 3 · match 2 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.B.5 Word order of time, place, manner and frequency phrases.** Rule: frequency words go before the main verb (after *be*); longer time and place phrases go at the end in the order manner-place-time, or at the front with a comma.
  - Must cover: "She worked carefully at home yesterday"; fronted phrase + comma (required from four words; optional shorter — Chicago 6.31, accepted either way); still/already/yet positions; *sometimes/usually* may open a sentence; never split verb and object ("I speak English well"); two adverbials of the same kind: smaller before larger ("at 6 on Monday").
  - Examples: "In 1998, the company opened its first store in Texas." / "My mother usually calls me on Sunday evenings." / "He speaks Spanish fluently at work." ESL errors: verb-object split (Spanish, Italian, French, Arabic); German time-manner-place ("I go tomorrow with the bus to work"); adverb after the object at the end only (Chinese).
  - Exercises (16): mc 2 · gap 3 · order 5 · error-spot 3 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · say-it 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Building mix; bank 110 (22 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (10 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Prepositions & Adverbials — Building Certificate.

### 8 · Connecting (B1) — 5 lessons, 90 exercises, bank 110

- **8.C.1 Adverb clauses of time, reason, condition and contrast.** Rule: an adverb clause is a whole subject-plus-verb group that answers *when, why, on what condition* or *despite what*; put a comma after it when it comes first, and usually none when it comes last.
  - Must cover: when, while, before, after, until, as soon as, once, because, since, as, if, unless, although, even though, though; comma after a fronted clause (OWL); a comma before a closing *although/whereas* contrast clause is allowed (Chicago, OWL — accepted either way); present tense for future in time/condition clauses ("when I arrive"); *since* = time or reason; *while* = time or contrast; reduced clause preview ("while waiting").
  - Examples: "As soon as the results arrive, the nurse will call you." / "We closed early because the power went out." / "Although the loan was approved, the rate was high." ESL errors: *will* inside when/if clauses (most L1s); "although..., but" (Chinese, Arabic, Korean, Vietnamese); "because..., so" (Chinese); comma before every *because/that* (Russian, German, Polish).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 1.
- **8.C.2 Prepositional phrases and subject-verb agreement.** Rule: the verb agrees with the main noun of the subject, not with a noun inside a phrase that follows it.
  - Must cover: "The list of items **is**" (OWL); one of / each of / every one of + singular; a number of + plural vs the number of + singular; some/most/all/none of + verb agreeing with the noun after *of* (*none* singular or plural, both standard — Merriam-Webster); *along with / as well as / together with* do not make a plural (OWL); there is/are + first noun; sums and measures as one unit ("Ten dollars is enough").
  - Examples: "The cost of the tests was higher than expected." / "One of the nurses on the night shift is from Ghana." / "The manager, along with two assistants, is attending." ESL errors: agreement with the nearest noun (all L1s); *as well as* read as *and*; "the people is" (Spanish *la gente*, Portuguese, Italian, Arabic); no -s at all (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · click 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 4 · transform 2 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **8.C.3 Preposition or conjunction? because of / because, despite / although, during / while, due to, instead of, apart from, according to.** Rule: a preposition is followed by a noun or an -ing form; a conjunction is followed by a clause.
  - Must cover: because of + noun; despite / in spite of + noun or -ing (never a clause; *despite of* is wrong; *despite the fact that* + clause); during + noun; *due to* accepted anywhere as a preposition (Merriam-Webster, Chicago 5.250) though older style limits it to after *be* — mark; instead of + -ing; except (for); besides = in addition to; apart from / aside from; *according to* never with *me*.
  - Examples: "The clinic closed because of the storm." / "Despite the rain, the market stayed open." / "During the meeting, nobody checked their phone." ESL errors: "despite of" (blend with *in spite of*, all L1s); "because of" + clause; "during" + clause (Spanish *durante*, French *pendant que*); "according to me" (Hindi/Urdu, Chinese, French).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 4 · classify 2 · match 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **8.C.4 Phrasal verbs and prepositional verbs.** Rule: a phrasal verb is a verb plus a small word with a new meaning; separable ones may split around a noun and must split around a pronoun (*turn it off*); prepositional verbs never split (*listen to it*).
  - Must cover: separable (fill out, hand in, pick up, put off, turn on/off, look up, throw away) vs inseparable (look after, run into, get over, go through) vs three-part (put up with, look forward to + -ing, get along with); formal one-word equivalents for writing (postpone, tolerate, submit); stress on the particle (say-it); literal vs idiomatic senses.
  - Examples: "Please fill out the form and hand it in." / "She looks after her grandmother on weekends." / "We had to put the meeting off." ESL errors: "turn off it" (all L1s); "look forward to see" (all L1s); avoiding phrasal verbs, overusing Latinate verbs (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese); "look it after" (Chinese, Arabic).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.C.5 Prepositions at the end: questions and relative clauses.** Rule: in questions and relative clauses the preposition usually goes at the end; putting it before *whom/which* is formal and also correct.
  - Must cover: "Who are you waiting for?" (neutral) vs "For whom are you waiting?" (formal); terminal prepositions are correct — the ban is a myth (Merriam-Webster; Khan Academy) — mark; *whom* after a fronted preposition; never double the preposition ("the office in which I work in"); "Where are you from?"; phrasal verbs cannot be fronted; that/which/who may be omitted in a defining clause when the preposition stays at the end (link Section 3).
  - Examples: "What is this form for?" / "That is the doctor I talked to." / "The committee to which she reported met monthly." ESL errors: always fronting ("From where are you?" — French, Spanish, German, Russian formal habit); resumptive pronoun ("the man who I talked to him" — Arabic, Farsi, Hebrew); preposition dropped ("the job I applied" — Chinese, Vietnamese).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · order 2 · listen-choose 1 · say-it 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Connecting mix; bank 110 (22 per lesson; agreement items in 8.C.2 are cross-tagged to Section 5 so CP2 passages can reuse them); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (12 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Prepositions & Adverbials — Connecting Certificate.

### 8 · Expanding (B2) — 5 lessons, 95 exercises, bank 110

- **8.E.1 Setting off adverbial phrases with commas.** Rule: a phrase that introduces or interrupts the main sentence is set off with commas; a short closing phrase usually is not.
  - Must cover: comma after an introductory phrase of four or more words, or any length if misreading is possible (OWL Extended Rules); paired commas round interrupters; no single comma between subject and verb; no comma before a closing phrase unless it is a contrast or afterthought; nonrestrictive participial phrases set off; absolute phrases ("Her shift over, she went home"); the *restrictive* test (does removing it change who/what?).
  - Examples: "After three years of negotiation, the two towns merged their water systems." / "The bridge, despite its age, carries 40,000 cars a day." / "The invoice was paid in full on March 3." ESL errors: comma after the subject ("The new manager, started Monday" — Chinese, Arabic); missing comma after long fronted phrases (all L1s); comma before every phrase (Russian, German).
  - Exercises (19): mc 2 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 5 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **8.E.2 Dashes and parentheses for adverbial detail.** Rule: dashes make a detail stand out, parentheses make it quieter, commas are neutral — choose one pair and close it with the same mark.
  - Must cover: em dash closed up (Chicago 6.85, OWL, APA) vs spaced en dash (UK/AP) — mark, both accepted; never mix a dash with a closing comma; a whole sentence in parentheses keeps its period inside, a fragment puts it outside; no comma before an opening parenthesis; a dash before a summarising phrase after a list; at most one dash pair per sentence; typing an em dash on a phone (two hyphens auto-correct).
  - Examples: "The vaccine—developed in under a year—reached 60 countries." / "Interest rates (which had been near zero) rose sharply in 2022." / "Three things matter in a job search: skills, timing, and contacts—especially contacts." ESL errors: hyphen used as a dash (all L1s); spaced hyphens; mismatched pairs; parentheses round essential information.
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 4 · transform 5 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **8.E.3 Arranging phrases: misplaced and dangling modifiers.** Rule: put a describing phrase next to the word it describes, and make sure the sentence names the doer of an opening -ing or -ed phrase.
  - Must cover: dangling participles corrected by naming the doer or rewriting as a clause (OWL); placement of *only, almost, even, just* ("She earns only $50"; relaxed placement in speech noted — Chicago 5.186); squinting modifiers; keep subject and verb close; end-focus on the important idea (link 12.E); "There is/It is" openers pushed to the end position when needed.
  - Examples: "Having reviewed the X-ray, the doctor ordered a second scan." / "The company sold nearly all of its stores in Ohio last year." / "Only the manager can approve refunds." ESL errors: dangling -ing openers (all L1s; strong in Spanish/Portuguese gerund users); *only* misplaced; long subject-verb separation (German, Japanese, Korean).
  - Exercises (19): mc 2 · click 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 5 · transform 6 · order 1 · dictation 1.
- **8.E.4 Prepositions in formal register: in terms of, with regard to, regarding, in relation to, in addition to, on behalf of, in accordance with, by means of, in the event of, prior to, as opposed to.** Rule: formal writing uses longer preposition groups; use them exactly, and prefer the plain word when it means the same.
  - Must cover: complex prepositions and plain equivalents (prior to → before; in the event of → if; with regard to → about); *regarding/concerning*; overuse of *in terms of*; *compared to/with* both standard (Chicago prefers *with* for contrast — mark); *between* for two or more, *among* for a group (Merriam-Webster); *per* ("per year"; *as per* is business jargon, avoid); *in regard to* (not *in regards to*).
  - Examples: "In accordance with the new policy, all invoices are due within 30 days." / "The report compares 2020 with 2023 in terms of cost per patient." / "Prior to the merger, the two banks had separate systems." ESL errors: translated L1 connectors ("in the framework of", "at the level of" — French, Spanish, Portuguese); "in regards to"; "as per my knowledge" (Indian English); *between* restricted to two (hypercorrection).
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 2 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **8.E.5 Preposition + -ing, and *to* as a preposition.** Rule: after a preposition a verb takes -ing, even when the preposition is *to*; *to* + base verb shows purpose.
  - Must cover: the *to*-preposition test (replace with a noun: look forward to it); for + -ing (what a thing is for) vs to + base (why a person acts); by + -ing (method); without/before/after/instead of/despite + -ing; on + -ing (formal); *used to + base* (past habit) vs *be/get used to + -ing*; *prefer X to Y*; *object to, in favor of, committed to* + -ing.
  - Examples: "I look forward to meeting the team." / "She saved money by cooking at home." / "This app is for tracking expenses." ESL errors: "look forward to meet" (all L1s); "for to buy" (Spanish *para*, Portuguese); *used to go* for *be used to going*; "interested to learn" (standard *interested in learning*; *interested to hear/see* idiomatic — accepted).
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 5 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 1 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Expanding mix (the 4 transform items always include one dangling-modifier rewrite and one punctuation rewrite); bank 110 (22 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (12 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Prepositions & Adverbials — Expanding Certificate.

### 8 · Mastery (C1) — 4 lessons, 80 exercises, bank 104

- **8.M.1 Compounding prepositional phrases to condense.** Rule: turn several short sentences into one by changing some of them into prepositional phrases, keeping one main clause in view.
  - Must cover: "The study was done in 2019. It looked at 400 patients in three hospitals." → "A 2019 study of 400 patients in three hospitals..."; a limit of three chained *of/in/for* phrases before restructuring (writing-centre "of-chain" guidance); phrase vs relative clause choice; repeating the preposition in a series for clarity ("in London, in Paris, and in Rome" — Chicago 5.253); *with*-absolutes ("with costs rising"); front-loading vs end-focus (link 12.M).
  - Examples: "With a budget of $2 million and two university partners, the city rebuilt the clinic in eighteen months." / "The engineer, after a decade at a firm in Detroit, opened her own practice." / "By the end of the decade, most households in the region had electricity." ESL errors: unreadable *of...of...of* chains (Romance-L1 nominal style); agreement lost after condensing; wrong preposition inside the condensed phrase.
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 3 · transform 8 · classify 1 · order 2 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **8.M.2 Fronted adverbials and inversion for emphasis.** Rule: when a negative or limiting adverbial starts the sentence, the auxiliary comes before the subject; when a place phrase starts it, the whole verb may come before the subject.
  - Must cover: never, rarely, seldom, hardly...when, no sooner...than, not only...but also, only then/after/when/if, not until, under/in no circumstances, on no account, little, nowhere, at no time (Cambridge; British Council C1); *do* inserted in simple tenses; no inversion when *only/not* modifies the subject ("Not many people know"); locative inversion only with be/lie/stand/sit/come; *So great was the demand that...*; formal-persuasive register. Bank items are shared with 10.M.2 (same rule, different focus) and de-duplicated.
  - Examples: "Only after the audit did the board discover the losses." / "Rarely has a discovery changed medicine so quickly." / "Behind the museum stands the oldest house in the city." ESL errors: inversion after any fronted adverbial ("Yesterday went I" — German, Dutch); no inversion after *Not only*; inverting the main verb instead of adding *do* (Spanish, Italian, French).
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 7 · classify 1 · order 2 · next 1 · say-it 1.
- **8.M.3 Sentence adverbials that hedge, boost and frame.** Rule: a sentence adverbial comments on the whole statement — how sure, how general, or from what angle — and takes a comma when it opens the sentence.
  - Must cover: arguably, to some extent, in most cases, by and large, in principle, admittedly, notably, in particular, broadly speaking, in the long run, on balance, at first glance; boosters (*clearly, obviously*) used sparingly in argument; *hopefully* as a sentence adverb is standard (Merriam-Webster) though traditionalists object — mark; *actually* and *eventually* false friends; *at last* vs *finally* vs *lastly*; at the end vs in the end; one framing adverbial per sentence.
  - Examples: "To some extent, the rise in rents reflects higher building costs." / "The new drug is, arguably, the most important discovery of the decade." / "On balance, the merger benefited both towns." ESL errors: *actually* = currently (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, German); *eventually* = possibly (German *eventuell*, Spanish, Portuguese); missing comma after a fronted sentence adverb (Chinese, Arabic); "in the other hand" (Spanish).
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 4 · classify 3 · match 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **8.M.4 Preposition precision in complex noun phrases.** Rule: a noun made from a verb or adjective keeps a fixed preposition, and the preposition must match the noun, not the verb you are thinking of.
  - Must cover: 30 pairs (a rise/increase/fall **in** a thing vs an increase **of** an amount; the impact/effect **on**; a solution **to/for** (both standard); a reason **for**; a demand **for**; access **to**; insight **into**; an alternative **to**; the difference **between** X **and** Y; responsibility **for**; the ability **to**; a lack **of**; research **on/into**; an attitude **to/toward**; a preference **for**; an opinion **of/about/on**); *between...and* never *between...to*; *from...to*.
  - Examples: "The report shows a 12% increase in energy costs and an increase of $300 in the average bill." / "There is no simple solution to the housing shortage." / "The study gives insight into the effect of sleep on memory." ESL errors: "impact to", "effect to" (Chinese, Hindi/Urdu, Arabic); "between... to" (Hindi/Urdu, Arabic, Farsi); "reason of" (Spanish, Arabic); "research about" (informal; accepted).
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · gap 6 · error-spot 4 · transform 3 · classify 1 · match 2 · dictation 1.

## Section 9 — Conjunctions & clause combining

Spiral: F and, but, or, so [VA24 2.LU.1] → B because, when, if; first subordinators [CA PII.9-10.6.Em; TEKS 5.11.D.viii] → C subordinating/correlative conjunctions, conjunctive adverbs; reason & concession [TEKS 7.10.D.iii, vi; CA PII.6.6.Br] → E parallel structure, appositives, main/subordinate clause punctuation [VA24 9.LU.1.A, D; 9.LU.2.A] → M deliberate clause variation for logical relationships [WIDA-LC 9-12 L6]. Modal and conditional *meaning* stays in Section 6; Section 9 owns clause joining and its punctuation.

### 9 · Foundations (A1) — 3 lessons, 45 exercises, bank 100

- **9.F.1 and, or with words and lists.** Rule: *and* adds, *or* gives a choice; in a list of three or more, put a comma between the items and *and* or *or* before the last one.
  - Must cover: two words joined with and/or take no comma; three or more take commas plus the serial comma (taught; its omission accepted when the list is clear — Chicago/MLA/APA vs AP); *or* after a negative ("I don't drink coffee or tea"); a compound subject takes a plural verb (link Section 5); *both* previewed.
  - Examples: "My sister and I work at the hospital." / "Do you want tea or coffee?" / "We need milk, bread, and eggs." ESL errors: *and* between every item (Arabic *wa*); "I don't like tea and coffee" (Spanish, Portuguese, Russian); singular verb after *X and Y* (Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · order 2 · match 1 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **9.F.2 Joining two sentences with and, but, so.** Rule: *and* adds, *but* contrasts, *so* gives the result; put a comma before the joining word when it joins two full sentences.
  - Must cover: comma before and/but/so between two complete sentences (OWL; Chicago 6.22), no comma when the second part has no subject ("She works at night and sleeps in the day"); *so* = as a result (not *so* = very); a chain of *and...and...and* split into sentences; sentence-initial *But/And* accepted in informal writing (Chicago), taught as a joiner inside the sentence at this band.
  - Examples: "I wanted to call, but my phone was dead." / "The bus was late, so I took a taxi." / "He cooks and she cleans." ESL errors: comma splice ("I was tired, I went home" — Chinese, Russian, Spanish, Arabic); *and* run-ons (Arabic); *but* after *although* (Chinese, Arabic, Korean — treated fully in 9.B.4).
  - Exercises (15): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 3 · order 3 · error-spot 1 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **9.F.3 Giving a reason with because.** Rule: *because* gives the reason, and it comes after the result.
  - Must cover: "I'm tired because I work at night"; spoken short answer "Why?" — "Because I'm sick." accepted in speech, attached to a sentence in writing; never *because...so* together; *because of* + noun previewed for reading only; *why* questions (link Section 10).
  - Examples: "She is happy because she has a new job." / "Why are you late? — Because the bus was late." / "We stayed home because it was raining." ESL errors: "because..., so" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai, Korean); *Because* fragments in writing (all L1s); *for* used for *because* (Spanish *por*, Portuguese).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · order 2 · match 1 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Foundations mix; bank 100 (34/33/33 across the three lessons); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (8 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Conjunctions & Clause Combining — Foundations Certificate.

### 9 · Building (A2) — 5 lessons, 80 exercises, bank 110

- **9.B.1 Reason and result: because, so, so that — clause order and the comma.** Rule: *because* starts the reason, *so* starts the result; a *because* clause can go first with a comma, but *so* can only sit between the two parts.
  - Must cover: "Because it rained, we stayed in" (comma) vs "We stayed in because it rained" (no comma; a comma before *because* only when the main clause is negative or the sentence would otherwise be ambiguous — CMOS Q&A, mark); *so* coordinator (comma before) vs *so that* purpose (+ can/will); *that's why*; *as/since* for reason previewed as formal (9.C.1); fragment "Because it was late."
  - Examples: "Because the pharmacy was closed, we drove to the hospital." / "The pharmacy was closed, so we drove to the hospital." / "I take the early train so that I can open the shop." ESL errors: "because..., so" (Chinese, Vietnamese); *so* for *so that*; *for* = because (Spanish, Portuguese; French *car* is standard but formal — mark); comma before every *because* (Russian, German, Polish).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **9.B.2 Time clauses: when, while, before, after, until, as soon as.** Rule: a time clause says when the main action happens; use the present tense in it even when the meaning is future.
  - Must cover: fronted time clause + comma; "When I arrive, I'll call" (no *will* in the clause); *while* + continuous; *before/after* + clause or + -ing; *until*; *as soon as*; past pairs "When the phone rang, I was cooking"; *when* (certain) vs *if* (uncertain).
  - Examples: "When the doctor calls, write down the appointment time." / "I'll wait here until the bus comes." / "After we finished the inventory, we locked the store." ESL errors: *will* in time clauses (Spanish, French, Arabic, Chinese, Turkish); *when* for *if* (German *wenn*, Dutch); "before to leave" (Spanish *antes de*, Italian); "until" for *by* (German *bis*).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **9.B.3 Condition: if, unless (joining and punctuation).** Rule: *if* opens the condition; use the present tense in the *if* clause and *will* or a command in the result.
  - Must cover: comma after a fronted *if* clause; *unless* = if not (never *unless...not*); *if* vs *when*; zero conditional for facts and instructions ("If the light is red, stop"); *in case* previewed; conditional meaning and modal choices are Section 6 — this lesson trains joining only.
  - Examples: "If you pay by Friday, you get a 5% discount." / "Unless the road is closed, the delivery arrives at noon." / "Call me if the machine stops." ESL errors: "If it will rain" (most L1s); "unless... not" (Spanish, Chinese); missing comma after a fronted *if* clause; *if* + *would* in the condition (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **9.B.4 Contrast: but, although, even though, though.** Rule: *but* joins two full sentences with a comma; *although* or *even though* starts a clause that can go first (with a comma) or last.
  - Must cover: never *although...but* in one sentence; *though* at the end in speech ("It was expensive, though."); *even though* stronger than *although*; *However,* previewed as a sentence opener (full treatment 9.C.2); *despite* + noun previewed (8.C.3).
  - Examples: "Although the job pays well, the commute is long." / "The commute is long, but the job pays well." / "He passed the test even though he was sick." ESL errors: "although..., but" (Chinese, Arabic, Korean, Vietnamese, Farsi, Turkish); *although* + noun ("although the rain"); *even* alone for *even though* (Chinese).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **9.B.5 Fragments, run-ons and comma splices when you join clauses.** Rule: every sentence needs one full main clause; a *because/when/if/although* clause alone is a fragment, and two main clauses need a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a joining word.
  - Must cover: fragment repair (attach it or drop the subordinator); run-on and comma-splice repair — period, comma + and/but/so, or make one clause subordinate (OWL); *then* is not a joining word ("I ate, then I slept" → "and then"); *also* is not a conjunction; one idea per sentence at this band.
  - Examples: "The clinic closed early. It was a holiday." → "The clinic closed early because it was a holiday." / "We finished the shift, and then we went home." / "When the manager arrived." → "When the manager arrived, the meeting started." ESL errors: comma splices (Chinese, Spanish, Russian, Arabic, French — L1 comma norms); *and then* splices; *Because* fragments (all L1s).
  - Exercises (16): mc 2 · gap 2 · order 2 · error-spot 4 · transform 4 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Building mix (the 2 error-spot items always include one splice/fragment); bank 110 (22 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (10 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Conjunctions & Clause Combining — Building Certificate.

### 9 · Connecting (B1) — 6 lessons, 108 exercises, bank 114

- **9.C.1 Subordinators by meaning: time, reason, purpose, condition, contrast, place, manner.** Rule: a subordinating conjunction makes its clause depend on the main clause and tells the reader what the logical link is.
  - Must cover: time (once, whenever, as, by the time, since, as long as), reason (since, as, now that), purpose (so that, in order that), condition (unless, provided that, as long as, in case, only if), contrast (whereas, while, even if), place (where, wherever), manner (as if, as though); *since/as/while* ambiguity; *in case* ≠ *if*; *even if* ≠ *even though*; tense after *by the time*.
  - Examples: "Now that the loan is approved, we can order the equipment." / "Keep the receipt in case the printer fails." / "Whereas the old plant used coal, the new one runs on gas." ESL errors: *in case* = if (French, Italian, Spanish); *as* misread as *because*; *even if* for *even though* (Spanish *aunque*); *so that* without a modal.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.C.2 Conjunctive adverbs: however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, otherwise, meanwhile, consequently, instead, then.** Rule: a conjunctive adverb links two full sentences in meaning only, so it needs a semicolon or a period before it and usually a comma after it.
  - Must cover: "; however," (OWL); sentence-initial *However,* is standard (Chicago; the Strunk & White objection named as a myth — mark); mid-position with paired commas; the *however* comma splice; *therefore* vs *so* register; *otherwise* = if not; *then* and *thus* usually without a comma; *moreover/furthermore* formal.
  - Examples: "The tests were negative; however, the doctor ordered a follow-up." / "Prices rose 8% last year. Consequently, the council raised the bus fare." / "Pay by the 5th; otherwise, you lose the discount." ESL errors: *however* splice (all L1s); "but however"; "on the contrary" for *on the other hand* (Spanish, French, Italian); *moreover* in casual speech.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 4 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **9.C.3 Correlative conjunctions: both...and, either...or, neither...nor, not only...but also, whether...or.** Rule: correlatives come in pairs, and what follows each half must have the same form.
  - Must cover: parallel halves (OWL Parallel Structure); with *either/or* and *neither/nor* the verb agrees with the nearer subject (OWL, Chicago; some usage guides accept the plural — mark); *neither...nor* never *neither...or*; *not only...but also* (comma optional; *also* may be dropped); *both* for two only; inversion after fronted *Not only* previewed (8.M.2/10.M.2).
  - Examples: "Either the landlord or the tenants are responsible for the repair." / "The vaccine was not only cheap but also easy to store." / "Neither the bank nor the credit union offers that rate." ESL errors: "neither... or"; non-parallel halves ("both fast and it is cheap"); "not only... but" + full new subject without *also*; agreement with the far subject.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 4 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **9.C.4 Cause and result across clause types: because/since/as; so...that; such...that; as a result; because of/due to.** Rule: choose the link by what follows it — a clause after *because/since/as*, a noun after *because of/due to*, and a full new sentence after *as a result* or *therefore*.
  - Must cover: *so* + adjective + *that* vs *such* + noun + *that*; *too...to / enough to* (link Section 7); *thus/hence* formal; "the reason is that" (not *because* — *the reason is because* is common speech, Merriam-Webster; taught as *that*, mark); *for* as a formal conjunction; *that* omitted in speech after *so*.
  - Examples: "The storm was so strong that the bridge closed." / "It was such a good offer that we signed the same day." / "Sales fell; as a result, the store cut its hours." ESL errors: "the reason is because" (all L1s); "so...that" without *that* in writing; "because of" + clause; "so much" + adjective (Spanish *tan*).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.C.5 Concession: although, even though, though, while, whereas, despite/in spite of, no matter, however + adjective.** Rule: concession admits one fact, then states the surprising other fact — a clause after *although/though/while*, a noun after *despite*.
  - Must cover: *although* (conjunction) vs *however* (adverb); *while/whereas* contrast; *despite the fact that* + clause; *no matter how/what*; *however hard* + clause; *yet* as coordinator; *still*; concession first for argument rhythm (link 12.E).
  - Examples: "While the treatment works, it is expensive." / "No matter how early we leave, the traffic is bad." / "Despite two warnings, the driver kept speeding." ESL errors: "although..., but"; "despite of"; "in spite of" + clause; "even" alone for *even though* (Chinese); *however* used as a conjunction.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.C.6 Sentence combining: from short sentences to one complex sentence.** Rule: make the idea you want the reader to focus on the main clause, and turn the other ideas into subordinate clauses or phrases.
  - Must cover: choosing the main clause; never two subordinators in a row ("Although because..."); one subordinate clause per sentence at this band, two at Expanding; tense kept consistent; varied sentence openings [VA24 8.LU.1.D]; read aloud to check rhythm.
  - Examples: "The nurse was tired. She finished her shift. She wrote the report." → "Although the nurse was tired, she wrote the report after finishing her shift." / "The town has one clinic. It serves 20,000 people." → "The town's one clinic serves 20,000 people." ESL errors: stacked subordinators; a fragment left behind; meaning changed while joining; every sentence starting with the subject (Chinese, Arabic).
  - Exercises (18): mc 1 · gap 2 · error-spot 2 · transform 9 · order 2 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Connecting mix (the 3 transform items always include one sentence-combining task); bank 114 (19 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (12 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Conjunctions & Clause Combining — Connecting Certificate.

### 9 · Expanding (B2) — 5 lessons, 95 exercises, bank 110

- **9.E.1 Parallel structure in lists, pairs and comparisons.** Rule: items joined by *and, or, but, than* or *as* must have the same grammatical form.
  - Must cover: -ing lists, infinitive lists (repeat *to* for all or for none), noun lists, clause lists; parallel after correlatives; comparisons with *than/as*; parallel bullet points and headings; faulty-parallelism repair (OWL); articles and prepositions repeated consistently.
  - Examples: "The job requires driving, lifting, and speaking with customers." / "She would rather retrain than lose her job." / "The program is open to workers who were laid off, who work part-time, or who have never held a job." ESL errors: mixed forms ("to swim, running, and bike" — all L1s); dropped articles in lists (Slavic, Chinese, Japanese); non-parallel correlatives.
  - Exercises (19): mc 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 6 · classify 1 · next 1 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.E.2 Appositives: restrictive and nonrestrictive.** Rule: an appositive renames the noun before it; put commas around it if the noun is already clear without it, and no commas if it is needed to say which one.
  - Must cover: "my brother Luis" (one of several) vs "my brother, Luis," (only one); appositives with dashes or a colon; opening and closing appositives; relative-clause reduction ("who is the CEO" → ", the CEO,"); the verb agrees with the head noun, not the appositive; titles and names in apposition [VA24 9.LU.1.D].
  - Examples: "Marie Curie, the first person to win two Nobel Prizes, worked in Paris." / "The novelist Toni Morrison taught at Princeton." / "We chose the cheaper option: a used van." ESL errors: comma inside a restrictive appositive; missing closing comma (all L1s); *which is* kept needlessly; appositive agreeing with the verb.
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 4 · transform 5 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.E.3 Punctuating main and subordinate clauses; compound-complex sentences.** Rule: a comma joins a fronted subordinate clause to its main clause; a semicolon joins two main clauses without a conjunction; a comma before *and/but/so* joins two main clauses with one.
  - Must cover: compound-complex = two main clauses + at least one subordinate [CA PII.7.6.Br; VA24 8.LU.1.A]; comma before a closing nonrestrictive *which* clause, none before a restrictive *that* clause — Chicago 5.250 prefers *that* for restrictive, British usage and Merriam-Webster allow *which* — mark, accepted; semicolon before a conjunctive adverb; no comma when *and* joins two verbs; commas and semicolons "to distinguish and divide main and subordinate clauses" [VA24 9.LU.2.A].
  - Examples: "Although the clinic opened at 7, the line had formed by 6, and the nurses were already inside." / "The city repaired the bridge, which had been closed since March; traffic returned the next day." / "Applicants must file by June 1, or they must wait a year." ESL errors: comma before *that* clauses (Russian, German, Polish, Turkish); no comma before nonrestrictive *which*; semicolon before a dependent clause; *which* without a comma for extra information.
  - Exercises (19): mc 2 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 5 · transform 5 · classify 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.E.4 Reduced clauses: participle phrases and reduced relatives as combiners.** Rule: when two clauses share a subject, the second can shrink to an -ing or -ed phrase — but the subject must stay the same.
  - Must cover: *Having finished...* (earlier action); *-ed* passive reductions ("Built in 1902, the bridge..."); reduced relatives ("the people living nearby", "the report published last year"); *while/when* + -ing; *with* + noun + -ing absolutes; dangling danger (cross-tagged to 8.E.3); *being* overuse; formal register.
  - Examples: "Having paid off the loan, the couple bought a second truck." / "The vaccine, developed in Oxford, was approved in December." / "Workers earning under $30,000 qualify for the credit." ESL errors: dangling reductions (all L1s); tense lost in reduction ("Finishing the exam, he had left"); *being* + past participle chains; reduced clause with a different subject (Spanish, Portuguese gerund habit).
  - Exercises (19): mc 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 7 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.E.5 Relative clauses as sentence combiners; sentential *which*.** Rule: a relative clause adds information about a noun inside another sentence — no commas and *that* or *who* when it identifies, commas and *which* or *who* when it only adds.
  - Must cover: who/whom/whose/which/that/where/when; omission of an object relative pronoun; preposition + which/whom (link 8.C.5); *which* referring to a whole clause (", which surprised everyone") — accepted, avoided when ambiguous (writing-centre guidance); *whose* for things is standard (Merriam-Webster); one relative clause per sentence in drafts; no nested relatives.
  - Examples: "The engineer who designed the bridge also planned the tunnel." / "The city's oldest bakery, which opened in 1921, closed last month." / "She retired early, which allowed her to care for her father." ESL errors: resumptive pronouns (Arabic, Farsi, Hebrew, Chinese); *which* for people; *the man which*; *that* after a comma; "what" as a relative ("the car what I bought" — some L1s and dialects).
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 5 · classify 1 · dictation 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Expanding mix (the 4 transform items include one appositive, one parallel-structure repair and one clause reduction); bank 110 (22 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (12 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Conjunctions & Clause Combining — Expanding Certificate.

### 9 · Mastery (C1) — 4 lessons, 80 exercises, bank 104

- **9.M.1 Clause variation for logic and emphasis: clefts, fronting, end-weight.** Rule: reorder the clauses so the information the reader needs comes first and the idea you want to stress comes last.
  - Must cover: it-cleft ("It was the nurses who noticed"); wh-cleft ("What the town needs is a second clinic"); fronting an object or complement for contrast; end-weight (the long clause last); *the more..., the more...*; fronted *so...that* with inversion (8.M.2); asyndeton and polysyndeton for rhythm (recognition); deliberate sentence-length variety [WIDA-LC 9-12 L6].
  - Examples: "What surprised the auditors was the size of the loss." / "It was not the price but the delay that cost the company the contract." / "The longer the bridge stayed closed, the more the town's shops suffered." ESL errors: cleft with the wrong tense agreement ("It was the nurses who notices"); "what I want is for to..."; fronting everything (Spanish, Italian topic-fronting habits); *the more...* without the second *the*.
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 7 · classify 2 · order 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.M.2 Subordination depth and readability.** Rule: subordinate to show logic, coordinate to show equal weight, and start a new sentence when a reader would have to hold more than two ideas at once.
  - Must cover: at most two levels of embedding; the centre-embedding trap ("The report that the committee that the mayor appointed wrote was long"); left- vs right-branching sentences; clause vs nominalisation (link Section 2); semicolons in a series of long items; a colon before an explaining clause; *and which* only after an earlier *which*; deliberate short sentences for emphasis.
  - Examples: "Because the study was small and its sample came from one clinic, its results, though striking, cannot be generalised." / "The mayor appointed a committee; the committee wrote a report; the report was long." / "The town, whose only bridge had failed inspection, applied for federal funds." ESL errors: page-long academic sentences (Spanish, Portuguese, Arabic, French essay styles); over-subordination; "and which"; comma chains instead of subordination (Chinese, Russian).
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 4 · transform 7 · classify 2 · order 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.M.3 Concession in argument: much as, as...as, albeit, for all, granted, admittedly, even so, that said, notwithstanding.** Rule: concede the opposing point in a subordinate clause or a short sentence, then put your own point in the main clause.
  - Must cover: "Much as I admire...", "Good as the plan is, ..." (fronted concessive), *albeit* + adjective or phrase (never a clause), *for all its faults*, *granted (that)*, *while acknowledging*, *notwithstanding* (before or after its noun); counter-claim → rebuttal moves [WIDA-LE Argue]; punctuation of fronted concessives; *however* vs *whereas* register; British Council C1 contrast set.
  - Examples: "Much as the plan saves money, it leaves the night shift uncovered." / "The bridge was rebuilt quickly, albeit at twice the estimate." / "Granted, the drug is expensive; even so, it cuts hospital stays in half." ESL errors: *albeit* + clause; "although..., but" resurfacing under pressure; *in spite of* + clause; *however* for every contrast; "on the contrary" for *by contrast*.
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 6 · classify 2 · match 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **9.M.4 Ellipsis and substitution in coordinated clauses.** Rule: when two joined clauses share words, you may leave the shared words out of the second one, as long as the reader can put them back exactly.
  - Must cover: gapping ("Ana studied law; her brother, medicine"); shared auxiliaries ("She can and will finish"); *do so / do too / so do I / neither do I*; *nor* with inversion; ellipsis after *than/as* and its ambiguity ("more than her brother"); *if so / if not*; *to* standing for a whole verb phrase ("I'd like to"); false ellipsis with non-matching forms ("has and will continue to" — Chicago 5.230s) .
  - Examples: "The first plant opened in 1950 and the second in 1962." / "He had not read the contract, nor had his lawyer." / "Some clinics will adopt the software, and others will not." ESL errors: dropping words that are not shared ("She can and will finishing"); "so I do" for *so do I*; full repetition instead of ellipsis (Chinese, Japanese, Korean); "also not" for *either/neither* (German *auch nicht*, Dutch).
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 6 · classify 2 · order 2 · match 1 · dictation 1.

## Section 10 — Questions, negatives & reported speech

Spiral: F yes/no & wh-questions, negatives [CI-A1] → B wh-questions in the past, question tags [CI-A2] → C complex tags, reported speech across tenses [CI-B1] → E reported speech extended, embedded questions [CI-B2] → M rhetorical questions and negative-adverbial inversion [CI-C1]. ### 10 · Foundations (A1) — 4 lessons, 60 exercises, bank 104

- **10.F.1 Yes/no questions with be and can.** Rule: to ask a yes/no question with *am/is/are* or *can*, put that word before the subject and end with a question mark.
  - Must cover: "Are you a nurse?" / "Is she at work?" / "Can you drive?"; short answers *Yes, I am. / No, she isn't. / Yes, I can.* (no contraction in a positive short answer); *Are there...?*; *Am I...?*; rising intonation (say-it); the question mark goes only at the end.
  - Examples: "Is the pharmacy open?" / "Are you from Ethiopia?" / "Can I pay by card?" ESL errors: statement + rising tone only ("You are a nurse?" — Spanish, Portuguese, Chinese, Russian, Arabic); "Yes, I'm."; *be* missing ("You nurse?" — Arabic, Russian, Chinese, Turkish); "You can drive?".
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · match 2 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.F.2 Yes/no questions with do and does.** Rule: with other verbs, start the question with *do* or *does* and keep the main verb in its base form.
  - Must cover: "Do you work on Sundays?" / "Does she speak Spanish?" (no -s on the main verb); short answers *Yes, I do. / No, she doesn't.*; "Do you have...?" (US) vs "Have you got...?" (UK, accepted); *Do you like* + -ing or noun; never "Do you are...?".
  - Examples: "Do you take the bus?" / "Does the clinic close at 5?" / "Do they sell phone cards?" ESL errors: no auxiliary ("You work on Sundays?" — most L1s); "Does she speaks" (all L1s); "Do you are tired?" (Spanish, Arabic); "Have you a car?" (Hindi/Urdu, French).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · match 2 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.F.3 Wh-questions: what, where, who, when, why, how, which, whose, how much/many, how old.** Rule: question word first, then *do/does/is/are/can*, then the subject, then the main verb.
  - Must cover: the question-word + auxiliary + subject + verb pattern; "What's your name?"; "Where do you live?"; "How much is it?" vs "How many children do you have?"; "What time...?"; "What does X mean?" (never "What means X?"); "How do you spell...?"; *who* as subject ("Who works here?", no *do*) shown for recognition, taught in 10.B.1.
  - Examples: "Where do you work?" / "What time does the bank open?" / "How much does the ticket cost?" ESL errors: "What means this word?" (Spanish, French, German, Russian, Italian); "Where you live?" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic); "How many money?" (most L1s); "How is your name?" (Portuguese, Spanish *cómo*).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · match 2 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.F.4 Negatives with not; one negative word only.** Rule: make a negative with *not* after *be* or *can* and with *don't/doesn't* before other verbs, and use only one negative word in a sentence.
  - Must cover: contractions isn't/aren't/don't/doesn't/can't; *I'm not* (never "amn't"); *doesn't* + base verb; *no* + noun vs *not* ("I have no time" / "I'm not busy"); *never* with a positive verb; negative imperatives ("Don't touch"); *any* after a negative; double negatives are nonstandard [VA17 4.8.b].
  - Examples: "She doesn't work on Fridays." / "I can't find my glasses." / "We don't have any milk." ESL errors: "I no like" (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Chinese); "I don't know nothing" (Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French, Russian, Polish — grammatical double negation in L1); "She doesn't works"; "I don't can" (Spanish, Arabic); *no* directly before the verb (Chinese *bu*).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Foundations mix (the listen-choose items pair a question with its natural short answer); bank 104 (26 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (8 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Questions, Negatives & Reported Speech — Foundations Certificate.

### 10 · Building (A2) — 5 lessons, 80 exercises, bank 110

- **10.B.1 Questions in the past; subject questions with who and what.** Rule: past questions start with *did* and keep the main verb in its base form — unless *who* or *what* is the subject, when there is no *did*.
  - Must cover: "Where did you go?" / "Did she call?"; *was/were* questions without *did*; "Who called?" vs "Who did you call?"; "What happened?"; "How long did it take?"; "Why didn't you...?" previewed (10.B.3).
  - Examples: "When did you move to Texas?" / "Who paid the bill?" / "Did the manager sign the form?" ESL errors: "Did you went?" (all L1s); "Who did call?"; "Where you went?" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic); "What did happen?".
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.B.2 Question tags, basic.** Rule: to check something, add a short tag — negative after a positive sentence, positive after a negative one — using the same auxiliary.
  - Must cover: "It's cold, isn't it?" / "You don't drive, do you?" / "She works here, doesn't she?" / "They didn't come, did they?"; "I'm late, aren't I?" (standard; *amn't I* Scottish/Irish only — mark); "Let's go, shall we?"; "Open the window, will/could you?"; *right?* as an informal universal tag in American speech (accepted in speech, never in writing); falling tone = checking, rising tone = real question (say-it).
  - Examples: "The clinic opens at 8, doesn't it?" / "You haven't paid yet, have you?" / "It was a long shift, wasn't it?" ESL errors: one universal tag (*isn't it?/no?* — Hindi/Urdu, Arabic, French *n'est-ce pas*, Spanish *¿no?*, Chinese); wrong auxiliary ("She works here, isn't she?"); negative sentence + negative tag (Japanese, Korean).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 5 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 2 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.B.3 Negative questions and how to answer them.** Rule: a negative question (*Don't you...? Isn't it...?*) expects agreement or shows surprise; answer *yes* if the positive fact is true and *no* if it is not, whatever the form of the question.
  - Must cover: "Don't you like it?" — "Yes, I do / No, I don't" (the answer follows the fact); "Why don't we...?" suggestions; "Isn't she the manager?" (surprise); "Aren't you cold?"; formal full form "Is she not...?"; "Why not?".
  - Examples: "Don't you have a key?" — "No, I don't." / "Why don't we take the earlier train?" / "Isn't the office closed on Mondays?" ESL errors: reversed yes/no answers (Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Hindi/Urdu, Swahili, Farsi — L1 answers agree with the statement); "Why you don't...?" (Spanish, Portuguese); "Do not you like...?" (Arabic, Chinese).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.B.4 Questions with a preposition at the end; How + adjective/adverb; What kind of / Which.** Rule: keep the preposition at the end of the question, and use *How* + a word to ask about length, distance, frequency or age.
  - Must cover: "Who are you waiting for?" / "What is it made of?" / "Where are you from?"; how long / far / often / old / tall / much + adjective; "What kind of...?" / "Which one?"; "What... for?" = why; "How about...?"; "How come...?" (informal, no inversion).
  - Examples: "Who did you talk to?" / "How often do you see the dentist?" / "How far is the station from here?" ESL errors: "From where are you?" (French, Spanish, German, Russian); dropped preposition ("Who are you waiting?" — Chinese, Vietnamese); "How long time?" (Spanish, French); "How many years do you have?" (Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.B.5 Negatives in the past and with modals; no/not/never/any; too/either.** Rule: past negatives use *didn't* + base verb, modal negatives put *not* after the modal, and *either* (not *too*) agrees with a negative.
  - Must cover: *didn't go* (never "didn't went"); wasn't/weren't; couldn't, won't, shouldn't; *mustn't* vs *don't have to* (meaning in Section 6); *never* + positive verb; *no* + noun = *not any*; nobody/nothing/nowhere with a positive verb; "I don't like it either" vs "I like it too"; *not... anymore / no longer* previewed.
  - Examples: "We didn't receive the invoice." / "She couldn't find parking." / "I don't have any cash, and my husband doesn't either." ESL errors: "didn't went" (all L1s); "I don't like it too" (Spanish, Chinese, Arabic); "nobody didn't come" (Spanish, Russian, Polish, Italian); "I have not a car" (French, German, Dutch).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1 · say-it 1.

### 10 · Connecting (B1) — 5 lessons, 90 exercises, bank 110

- **10.C.1 Complex question tags.** Rule: the tag copies the first auxiliary of the sentence (or uses *do/does/did* when there is none) plus the pronoun that matches the subject.
  - Must cover: modals ("can't she?", "won't they?", "would you?"); *have* as a main verb ("You have a car, don't you?" US; "haven't you?" UK, accepted); "there is..., isn't there?"; nobody/nothing → *they/it* ("Nobody called, did they?"); "used to..., didn't you?"; "had better..., hadn't you?"; "ought to..., shouldn't you?" (*oughtn't* accepted); *this/that* → *it*; same-polarity tags for interest ("So you're leaving, are you?"); intonation meaning (say-it) [CI-B1].
  - Examples: "You'd rather work nights, wouldn't you?" / "Nothing was stolen, was it?" / "There's a pharmacy nearby, isn't there?" ESL errors: universal tags (see 10.B.2); "has she?" after "She has a car"; "does it?" after *there is*; "am I not?" is formal and accepted.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 5 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.C.2 Reported statements: backshift, pronouns, time and place words.** Rule: when you report in the past what someone said, move the verb one step back in time, and change the pronouns and time words to fit the new speaker.
  - Must cover: present simple → past simple; present continuous → past continuous; present perfect → past perfect; past simple → past perfect ("usually", British Council; unchanged also accepted); *will → would, can → could, may → might, must → had to* (obligation) / *must* (deduction); *should/would/could/might* unchanged; **no backshift** when the fact is still true or the reporting verb is present (British Council, Cambridge) — both answers accepted in keys; *that* optional; *say* (no object) vs *tell* + object ("said to me" ok, "said me" wrong); today → that day, tomorrow → the next/following day, yesterday → the day before, ago → before, here → there, this → that, now → then.
  - Examples: "'I'm tired,' Ana said." → "Ana said (that) she was tired." / "The nurse told me that the clinic opened at 8." (*opens* also accepted — still true) / "He said he had paid the bill the day before." ESL errors: "said me" (Spanish, French, Portuguese, Arabic, Hindi/Urdu); no backshift at all (Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic, Hebrew, Russian — L1 keeps the original tense); "told that" without an object; comma before *that* (Russian, Polish, German).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **10.C.3 Reported questions.** Rule: a reported question uses statement word order, no *do/does/did*, no question mark, and *if* or *whether* for a yes/no question.
  - Must cover: "She asked where I lived" (not "where did I live"); "He asked if/whether I had a car" (*if* more common; *whether* in formal writing and before *or not* — British Council); *wanted to know, wondered*; the question word is kept; backshift applies; a period at the end; requests reported with *asked + to* (10.C.4).
  - Examples: "'Where do you work?'" → "She asked me where I worked." / "'Did you lock the door?'" → "He asked whether I had locked the door." / "The doctor asked how long I had had the pain." ESL errors: question order kept ("asked where did I live" — all L1s, strongest in Spanish, Arabic, Chinese); question mark at the end; "asked that"; "if or not".
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 6 · classify 1 · order 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **10.C.4 Reported commands, requests and suggestions; say, tell, ask.** Rule: report an order or request with *tell/ask* + person + *to* (or *not to*), and a suggestion with *suggest* + -ing or *suggest that*.
  - Must cover: "told me to wait", "asked us not to smoke"; *ask* (request) vs *tell* (order); order/warn/advise/remind + object + *to*; *suggest/recommend* + -ing, or *that* + base verb (US subjunctive) / *that* + *should* (UK) — mark, both accepted; never "suggest me to" (Cambridge); offer/promise/refuse/agree + *to*; *ask for* + noun; *say* takes no personal object.
  - Examples: "The pharmacist told me to take one tablet with food." / "She asked him not to park there." / "The mechanic suggested replacing the battery." ESL errors: "suggest me to" (Spanish, Chinese, Arabic, French, Portuguese); "told to me"; "said him"; "ask to him" (Spanish *pedir a*); "advised to me".
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **10.C.5 Indirect (embedded) questions and polite forms.** Rule: inside a longer sentence — *Could you tell me..., Do you know..., I wonder...* — the question keeps statement word order, and the sentence ends with a question mark only if the whole sentence is a question.
  - Must cover: "Could you tell me where the bank is?" (frame is a question); "I wonder whether the office is open." (period — Grammarly; MLA); "Do you know what time it opens?"; "I'd like to know if..."; no *do/does/did*; politeness in customer service and email.
  - Examples: "Do you know where the elevator is?" / "I wonder if they accept credit cards." / "Could you tell me how long the wait is?" ESL errors: "Could you tell me where is the bank?" (all L1s; Spanish, Arabic, Chinese, Russian strongest); question mark after *I wonder if...*; "I don't know what does it mean" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 5 · order 1 · listen-choose 1 · say-it 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Connecting mix (the 3 transform items always include one reported statement and one reported question); bank 110 (22 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (12 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Questions, Negatives & Reported Speech — Connecting Certificate.

### 10 · Expanding (B2) — 5 lessons, 95 exercises, bank 110

- **10.E.1 Reporting verbs and their patterns.** Rule: each reporting verb takes a fixed pattern — verb + *that*, verb + object + *to*, verb + -ing, or verb + preposition + -ing — and the pattern must match the verb.
  - Must cover: *that*-clause verbs (claim, admit, deny, explain, insist, argue, point out, mention); object + *to* (advise, warn, urge, remind, persuade, encourage, invite); -ing (admit, deny, suggest, recommend, mention); preposition + -ing (accuse sb of, apologize for, blame sb for, congratulate sb on, insist on, thank sb for, warn sb against); "explain to me"; *recommend/demand/insist that* + base verb (mandative subjunctive, US) vs *should* (UK) — mark, both accepted [CI-B2].
  - Examples: "The auditor accused the firm of hiding losses." / "She denied having seen the memo." / "The board recommended that the CEO resign." ESL errors: "explain me", "suggest me to", "accuse him to steal", "recommend to do" (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Arabic, Chinese); "insist to" for *insist on* -ing; "warn him of" for *against*.
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 2 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **10.E.2 Reported speech across modals, conditionals and long stretches.** Rule: modals with a past form move back (*will → would, can → could, may → might, must → had to*); the others stay; conditionals and time references shift to the new time and place.
  - Must cover: *would/could/should/might/ought to* unchanged; *shall → would/should*; *must* obligation → *had to*, deduction → *must*; first conditional → past forms ("if it rained they would cancel"), second and third unchanged; here/this/now/tonight/next week shifts and when **not** to shift (same place or time); mixed reports (statement + question + command in one paragraph); varied reporting verbs across a summary.
  - Examples: "'I'll call you tomorrow.'" → "He promised he would call me the next day." / "'You must sign here.'" → "The clerk said I had to sign there." / "'If the test is positive, you'll need a scan.'" → "The doctor said that if the test was positive I would need a scan." ESL errors: invented back-shifted modals ("woulded"); *had to* for deductive *must*; shifting *here/now* when the speaker has not moved; tense drift across a paragraph (all L1s).
  - Exercises (19): mc 2 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 7 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **10.E.3 Reporting sources in academic and workplace writing.** Rule: report an author or document with a present-tense verb when the claim still stands (*Smith argues that...*) and a past-tense verb for a finished study or event (*Curie reported that...*), keeping the reported content in its own tense.
  - Must cover: neutral (state, note, report, observe), argumentative (argue, claim, contend, maintain), evaluative (acknowledge, concede, dismiss); MLA prefers the present for discussing a source, APA the past or present perfect for research findings — mark, both taught by register; *according to X* never followed by *X says*; hedged reporting (*suggests, appears to*); paraphrase vs quotation (link Section 13); *It is reported that / X is said to* previewed (10.M.4).
  - Examples: "The report notes that unemployment fell in 2023." / "Curie (1898) reported that the new element emitted radiation." / "According to the manual, the filter must be changed monthly." ESL errors: "according to X, he says" (all L1s); *the author says* for everything; tense clash inside the report; "in the article it says" (Spanish, Portuguese).
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 2 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **10.E.4 Negation in longer sentences: scope, transferred negation, not...either / neither...nor.** Rule: put the negative where the meaning is; *I don't think X is...* is the natural English form of *I think X is not...*.
  - Must cover: transferred negation with think/believe/expect/suppose; *not all* vs *all...not* ambiguity ("Not all the staff were trained"); *no longer / not anymore*; *not only / not even*; *neither...nor* agreement; hardly/scarcely/seldom are already negative; *any-* words after negatives; negative infinitives (*decided not to*; *to not* accepted informally — mark); *so/not* substitution ("I hope not", "I'm afraid so"); *no* + comparative.
  - Examples: "I don't think the invoice was sent." / "Not everyone qualifies for the credit." / "She decided not to renew the lease." ESL errors: "I hardly can't" (Spanish, Portuguese, Russian); "I hope no" (Spanish); "I think he doesn't" (accepted, less natural); *neither* + plural verb hypercorrection.
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 5 · classify 2 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **10.E.5 Question forms and register: direct, indirect, modal requests, questions in writing.** Rule: match the question form to the situation — direct with friends, indirect or modal with customers and strangers, and only rhetorical or research questions in essays.
  - Must cover: "Would you mind + -ing?", "Could you possibly...?", "I was wondering if you could..."; "Do you happen to know...?"; indirect questions end with a period (Grammarly; MLA); declarative questions in speech ("You're leaving?"); research questions ("This paper asks whether..."); a question in a title keeps its question mark; no tag questions in formal writing.
  - Examples: "Would you mind sending the invoice again?" / "I was wondering whether the position is still open." / "This report asks why the two clinics had such different outcomes." ESL errors: over-direct requests ("Send me the file." — Russian, German, Hebrew, Chinese directness norms); question mark after an indirect question; tags in formal email (Hindi/Urdu "isn't it?").

### 10 · Mastery (C1) — 4 lessons, 80 exercises, bank 104

- **10.M.1 Rhetorical questions and questions as structure in argument.** Rule: a rhetorical question expects no answer from the reader — use it sparingly to frame a point, and answer it yourself in the next sentence.
  - Must cover: at most one per section; the question mark stays; "Why does this matter?" as a transition; questions in headings (question mark kept, title case); the persuasive negative question ("Isn't it time we...?"); no strings of questions; aims stated as indirect questions in abstracts; reader-facing questions in manuals and instructions.
  - Examples: "Why did the vaccine succeed where earlier ones failed? The answer lies in its storage temperature." / "What, then, should the council do?" / "Is a four-day week realistic for hospitals?" ESL errors: a rhetorical question left as the argument (no answer given — common in Arabic, Spanish, Farsi rhetorical traditions); question marks after indirect questions; "Isn't it?" appended to claims.
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · gap 2 · error-spot 3 · transform 6 · classify 2 · match 1 · next 1 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.M.2 Inversion after negative and restrictive expressions.** Rule: when a negative or limiting expression opens the sentence, the auxiliary comes before the subject — *Never have I..., Not only did..., No sooner had...than*.
  - Must cover: the trigger list (Cambridge; British Council C1): never, rarely, seldom, hardly/scarcely/barely...when, no sooner...than, not only...but (also), only then/later/when/after/if/by, not until, under/in no circumstances, on no account, at no time, little, nowhere, neither/nor; *do*-support in simple tenses; no inversion when the negative modifies the subject only ("Not everyone agreed"); *Nor/Neither* continuing a negative; *So + adjective + be + subject*; formal-persuasive register. Same rule as 8.M.2 from the sentence-emphasis side; bank items shared and never drawn twice.
  - Examples: "Not only did the company miss the deadline, but it also lost the client." / "No sooner had the bridge reopened than the flooding returned." / "Under no circumstances should staff share passwords." ESL errors: no inversion ("Not only the company missed..." — all L1s); inverting the main verb ("Never went I" — Spanish, Italian, French, German); "no sooner...when"; a second negative after inversion (Spanish, Russian).
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 7 · classify 1 · order 2 · next 1 · say-it 1.
- **10.M.3 Negation for precision and tone: litotes, partial negation, negative prefixes, scope.** Rule: choose the negative form that says exactly how much you deny — a little (*not entirely*), a lot (*by no means*), or everything (*not at all*).
  - Must cover: litotes (*not uncommon, not without merit*) and its limit (one per paragraph); *by no means / in no way / far from*; partial negation (*not all, not every, not necessarily, not entirely*); prefixes un-, in-/im-/il-/ir-, dis-, non-, mis- and the traps *inflammable* (= flammable) and *disinterested* (both "impartial" and "uninterested" are recorded by Merriam-Webster — taught as *impartial*, mark); *cannot* vs *can not* (the two-word form only for contrast with *can*); lexical negation (*fail to, lack, absence of*); the scope of *only*.
  - Examples: "The results were not unexpected, but the scale was." / "The plan is by no means final." / "Not every patient responds, and not all responses last." ESL errors: litotes everywhere (French, Spanish academic habit); "can not" spacing; *disinterested* = bored; *inflammable* read as "not flammable" (all L1s); "un-" over-generalised (*unpossible*).
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 4 · classify 3 · match 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **10.M.4 Reported speech in formal reports: passive reporting, reporting nouns, free indirect style.** Rule: in formal writing you can report a claim without naming its source (*It is believed that... / X is thought to...*) or turn the report into a noun (*the claim that...*).
  - Must cover: *It is said/thought/believed/reported/estimated that...*; *X is said to be / to have been*; reporting nouns + *that*-clause or *of* + -ing (claim, suggestion, assertion, allegation, warning, promise, admission — Cambridge "Reported speech: reporting nouns"); *the fact that*; hedged reporting (*is understood to*); minutes and summaries ("The committee agreed that..."); free indirect speech in narrative (recognition only); consistent tense in a summary; avoiding *as X said* chains.
  - Examples: "The building is thought to date from 1780." / "The auditor's claim that the figures were altered was never proved." / "It was agreed that the contract would be renewed for one year." ESL errors: "It is said that X to be"; "is thought that"; reporting noun + *to* where *that/of* is needed ("the suggestion to leave" — accepted for some nouns, mark); *according to* + a full clause.
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 7 · classify 2 · match 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.

### 11 · Connecting (B1) — 7 lessons, 126 exercises, bank 112

- **11.C.1 Commas in compound sentences and after introductory elements.** Rule: put a comma before *and, but, so, or, yet, for, nor* when it joins two full sentences, and after an introductory clause or long phrase.
  - Must cover: FANBOYS between independent clauses (OWL); no comma before *and* joining two verbs; comma after a fronted dependent clause; after an introductory phrase (four+ words; shorter when misreading is possible — OWL/Chicago; both accepted); after *However, Yes, Well*; no comma before *because* unless needed (CMOS Q&A); splice and run-on repair (link 9.B.5) [TEKS 7.10.D.viii; VA24 8.LU.2.A].

- **11.C.2 Commas around nonrestrictive elements, interrupters, direct address, contrasts and dialogue tags.** Rule: use a pair of commas around information that could be removed without changing who or what you mean, and none around information that identifies.
  - Must cover: nonrestrictive *which* clause vs restrictive *that* clause (Chicago prefers *that*; *which* without commas accepted — Merriam-Webster); appositives (9.E.2); interrupters (*however, for example, of course*); direct address; contrast ("not X, but Y"); "Dallas, Texas, is..."; dialogue tags; never a single comma between subject and verb [TEKS 8.10.D.vi].
  - Examples: "Our neighbor, who is a nurse, helped." / "The nurse who lives next door helped." / "The new policy, of course, applies to everyone." ESL errors: missing closing comma (all L1s); commas round restrictive clauses (Russian, German, Polish); *that* after a comma.

- **11.C.3 Semicolons.** Rule: a semicolon joins two closely related full sentences without a joining word, and separates list items that already contain commas.
  - Must cover: between independent clauses (OWL); before a conjunctive adverb ("; however,"); never before a dependent clause or a fragment; never to introduce a list; in complex series ("Lima, Peru; Quito, Ecuador; and Bogotá, Colombia"); semicolon vs period; lowercase after it; rarely more than one per sentence [TEKS 7.10.D.viii].

- **11.C.4 Colons.** Rule: a colon comes after a complete sentence to introduce a list, an explanation or a quotation — never after a verb or a preposition.
  - Must cover: a complete sentence before the colon; not after *such as / including / are / for example*; before an explanation ("He had one goal: to finish"); before a formal quotation; times (3:30), ratios, title: subtitle, "Dear Sir or Madam:"; lowercase after a colon unless a proper noun, a quotation, or two or more sentences follow (Chicago, MLA) — APA capitalises a complete sentence after a colon; mark, accepted; one space after [TEKS 8.10.D.vi; VA17 10.7].
  - Examples: "The clinic offers three services: vaccines, blood tests, and X-rays." / "She had one rule for new drivers: never text at the wheel." / "The meeting starts at 9:15." ESL errors: colon after *are/including* (all L1s); colon for semicolon; capital after every colon (German); a space before the colon (French).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 4 · transform 3 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.C.5 Quotation marks with direct quotations and titles.** Rule: put a speaker's or writer's exact words in double quotation marks, keep commas and periods inside the closing mark, and use quotation marks for titles of short works.
  - Must cover: US placement — commas and periods inside; colons and semicolons outside; ? and ! by meaning (OWL); single marks inside double; introducing a quotation with a comma, a colon or *that* (no comma after *that*); partial quotations blend in without a comma; titles: quotation marks for articles, poems, songs, episodes; italics for books, films, newspapers (Chicago/MLA/APA; AP uses quotation marks for all — mark); scare quotes sparingly; quotation vs paraphrase (link Section 13) [VA17 7.8.e; VA24 8.LU.2.B].
  - Examples: "The manual says, "Change the filter every month."" / "The report called the plan "ambitious but underfunded."" / "She read "The Gift of the Magi" in *The Norton Anthology*." ESL errors: period outside (UK-trained, French, German, Spanish); « »; comma after *that*; capital on a partial quotation; quotation marks for every title.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 5 · transform 4 · classify 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.C.6 Commonly confused words: meaning pairs.** Rule: some pairs look or sound alike but do different jobs — learn each pair with a memory hook and check a dictionary when in doubt.
  - Must cover (40): affect/effect ("A is for action"), accept/except, advice/advise, then/than, lose/loose, quiet/quite, principal/principle, complement/compliment, stationary/stationery, passed/past, breath/breathe, choose/chose, lead/led, precede/proceed, personal/personnel, capital/capitol, cite/site/sight, desert/dessert, weather/whether, *a lot* (two words), *all right* (*alright* informal — Merriam-Webster), every day/everyday, maybe/may be, sometime/some time/sometimes, anymore/any more, apart/a part, altogether/all together, already/all ready, into/in to, farther/further (interchangeable for distance; *further* for "more" — Merriam-Webster), fewer/less (*less* accepted with money, time, distance — Merriam-Webster), who/whom, borrow/lend, bring/take, lie/lay, rise/raise, say/tell, make/do, e.g./i.e. [TEKS 7.10.D.ix, 8.10.D.vii].
  - Examples: "The new schedule affected everyone; the effect on morale was immediate." / "The principal reason was cost, not principle." / "There were fewer patients but less time." ESL errors: borrow/lend, say/tell, make/do, bring/take (one L1 verb — Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, French, Arabic, Chinese, Turkish); "advices"; "alot"; "loose" for *lose* (all L1s).
  - Exercises (18): mc 5 · gap 5 · error-spot 4 · match 2 · classify 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.C.7 Spelling: American and British forms, suffix rules, tricky words.** Rule: choose one spelling system (American in this course) and keep it throughout, and learn the few suffix rules that decide most hard words.
  - Must cover: US/UK pairs (color/colour, center/centre, organize/organise, traveled/travelled, program/programme, check/cheque, gray/grey, practice/practise, license/licence, defense/defence, catalog/catalogue; judgment preferred by Chicago; canceled/cancelled both US — Merriam-Webster); -able/-ible; -ance/-ence; -ceed/-cede/-sede (exceed, proceed, succeed; supersede); -ly after -e (completely; truly, duly, wholly); 30 tricky words (accommodate, occurrence, separate, definitely, necessary, receive, government, environment, occasion, embarrass, recommend, maintenance, privilege, questionnaire, rhythm, conscience/conscious, calendar, acknowledgment, liaison, restaurant, schedule, colleague, guarantee, immediately, beginning, business, achieve, believe, weird, sincerely); spellcheck limits [TEKS 5.11.D.xi; VA24 9.LU.2.C].
  - Examples: "The organization's office is in the city center." / "Please proceed to the second floor; the meeting will not exceed an hour." / "We definitely need separate calendars." ESL errors: US/UK mixing (Commonwealth-schooled learners: Indian, Pakistani, Nigerian, Kenyan, Caribbean); "definately", "seperate", "recieve", "goverment", "beleive" (all L1s); -tion/-sion confusion.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 4 · classify 2 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 3.

### 11 · Expanding (B2) — 6 lessons, 114 exercises, bank 114

- **11.E.1 Choosing among semicolons, colons, dashes and parentheses to set off clauses.** Rule: pick the mark by how much attention you want — a colon points forward, a semicolon balances, a dash interrupts loudly, and parentheses whisper.
  - Must cover: colon vs dash before an explanation (dash emphatic, colon formal); dash pair vs comma pair vs parentheses; semicolon vs comma + conjunction; no colon after a fragment; parentheses with citations (Section 13); brackets inside parentheses; one dash pair per sentence; punctuation after a closing parenthesis; consistency across a document [TEKS E1.9.D.v; E2.9.D.v].
  - Examples: "The vaccine had one drawback: it had to be kept at -70°C." / "The vaccine—cheap, stable, and easy to ship—reached rural clinics first." / "The trial (Phase 3, 30,000 participants) ended in November." ESL errors: colon after *such as*; a dash for every pause (Russian, Spanish); parentheses round essential information; semicolon before a fragment.
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 4 · transform 5 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.E.2 Hyphens, en dashes and em dashes.** Rule: a hyphen joins words that work together before a noun, an en dash shows a range, and an em dash sets off a break in thought.
  - Must cover: compound modifiers before a noun (well-known author, ten-year plan) but not after it, and never after an -ly adverb (highly paid — OWL); suspended hyphens (first- and second-year); prefixes (ex-, self-, all-; mid-August before a capital; *nonprofit* closed — Chicago/Merriam-Webster, mark); twenty-one to ninety-nine; fractions as adjectives; en dash for ranges (2010–2020, pp. 12–15; hyphen accepted informally); em dash closed (Chicago, APA, MLA) vs spaced (AP, UK) — mark; *decision-making* (adj.) vs *decision making* (n.) — dictionary check (Merriam-Webster lists both); *re-cover* vs *recover*.
  - Examples: "The 30-year-old bridge needs a long-term plan." / "The 2019–2023 data show a steady rise." / "Two towns—one rich, one poor—shared a single clinic." ESL errors: hyphen after -ly ("highly-paid"); *high-school* as a noun; spaced hyphens as dashes; hyphens in fixed open compounds ("ice-cream" — accepted UK).
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 4 · classify 2 · dictation 1.
- **11.E.3 Apostrophes in complex cases; plurals of letters, numbers and abbreviations.** Rule: form the possessive on the last word of a name or compound, share one *'s* when two people own one thing, and never use an apostrophe to make a plural.
  - Must cover: names ending in *s* (Jones's — Chicago 7.16, MLA, APA; Jones' — AP, accepted); plural names (the Joneses'); compounds (brother-in-law's); joint vs separate possession (Ana and Luis's house; Ana's and Luis's cars); possessive before a gerund ("his leaving early"); classical names and "for goodness' sake" (Chicago exceptions — mark); plurals of letters ("p's and q's" — Chicago allows the apostrophe for single lowercase letters only), 1990s, CEOs, '90s; contractions avoided in formal essays (APA/MLA allow them sparingly — mark).
  - Examples: "Dr. Jones's clinic opened in 1998." / "The two families' land bordered the river." / "The company hired three CEOs in the 1990s." ESL errors: "Jone's"; "the 1990's"; "CEO's" plural; "it's" possessive (all L1s); apostrophes on possessive pronouns ("her's").
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 4 · error-spot 5 · transform 2 · classify 1 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.E.4 Numbers, dates, times, money and percentages in text.** Rule: spell out small numbers and any number that starts a sentence; use numerals for exact measurements, money, times, percentages and dates.
  - Must cover: spell out one to nine, numerals from 10 (APA, AP — taught) vs one to one hundred spelled out (Chicago, humanities) vs "one or two words" (MLA) — mark, accepted by tagged style; never open a sentence with a numeral; numerals with units and money ($1,500; 3.5 km; 25%); *percent* vs % (APA: % with numerals; Chicago: *percent* in humanities — mark); times (3:30 p.m., noon, midnight; 24-hour clock accepted); dates (May 5, 2026; no *5th* in formal US text); the 1990s; large round numbers (2 million); ranges; consistency within a sentence; decimal point.
  - Examples: "Twelve nurses joined in 2024, and three more will start in May." / "The loan carries 6.5% interest over 15 years." / "The shift ends at 11:30 p.m." ESL errors: decimal comma and "1.500" (Europe, Latin America); a numeral opening a sentence; "25 %" spaced (French, German); "3:30 o'clock"; "the 5 of May" (Spanish, Portuguese, French).
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 4 · transform 4 · classify 2 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.E.5 Capitalisation in titles, headings and formal documents.** Rule: in a title or heading, capitalise the first and last words and every main word, keep short articles, conjunctions and prepositions lowercase, and follow the style manual in reference lists.
  - Must cover: headline style (Chicago 8.159, MLA: lowercase a/an/the, and/but/or/for/nor, prepositions of any length) vs APA title case (capitalise all words of four or more letters, prepositions included) — mark, Chicago/MLA taught, APA accepted in APA-tagged items; sentence case for APA reference titles (APA "Sentence case"); hyphenated compounds in titles; always a capital after a colon in a title; *President Lincoln* vs *the president* (Chicago); departments, courses, regions, eras, religions, languages; lowercase generic nouns; *internet* lowercase (Chicago 17, APA 7, Merriam-Webster; *Internet* accepted).
  - Examples: "Her article "The Cost of Free Parking" appeared in *The Atlantic*." / "APA reference: The cost of free parking." / "President Obama met the mayor; the president spoke first." ESL errors: Every Word Capitalised (German; over-generalised title case); lowercase proper adjectives (Romance L1s); capitalised job titles after names (Spanish, Portuguese, corporate habit).
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · click 3 · gap 2 · error-spot 5 · transform 3 · classify 2 · dictation 1.
- **11.E.6 Spelling with reference tools: word families, roots, Latin and Greek plurals.** Rule: check any word you are unsure of in a dictionary, not only with a spellchecker, and use word families to predict spelling.
  - Must cover: phone dictionary lookup (Merriam-Webster, Oxford Learner's); spellcheck blind spots (homophones, proper nouns, wrong-word errors); families and suffix stability (-tion/-sion/-cian; -ous/-ious; -ant/-ent); roots (bio, geo, graph, port, dict, scrib, -logy); silent-letter families (sign/signature; muscle/muscular); doubling in longer words (occurred, referred; offered, benefited — US single *l*); -ize consistency; Latin/Greek plurals (criteria/criterion, phenomena, analyses, theses, media; *data* singular standard in general writing — Merriam-Webster; plural required in APA — mark); names from other scripts transliterated one way [VA24 9.LU.2.C; CEFR-OC B2].
  - Examples: "The committee's criteria were clear, but its single most important criterion was cost." / "The data is consistent with earlier analyses." (APA register: *data are*) / "The town referred applicants to the new office that benefited them." ESL errors: "criterias", "phenomenas" (all L1s); "occured", "refered"; "benefitted" (UK, accepted); -ise/-ize mixing; own-name spelling variation (Arabic, Chinese, Russian, Amharic scripts).

### 11 · Mastery (C1) — 5 lessons, 100 exercises, bank 110

- **11.M.1 Punctuating quotations to a style manual: run-in vs block, ellipses, brackets, sic, citations.** Rule: a short quotation runs into your sentence inside quotation marks; a long one becomes an indented block without quotation marks; show every change with brackets or an ellipsis, and place the citation where the manual says.
  - Must cover: block thresholds — MLA more than four lines of prose, APA 40 or more words, Chicago about 100 words or eight lines — mark, taught per tagged style; run-in citation before the period ("...end" (Smith 45).); block citation after the final period; ellipsis for omission (three spaced periods, four at a sentence end — MLA/Chicago; APA three or four periods); brackets for insertions and changed capitals ([T]he — MLA/Chicago; APA allows a silent change of the first letter — mark); [sic]; single inside double; quoted dialogue (MLA Style Center); introduction with a colon, a comma or *that*; no "dropped" quotations [VA24 11.LU.2; VA17 12.7-12.8].
  - Examples: "Carson warned that "the most alarming of all man's assaults upon the environment is the contamination of air, earth, rivers, and sea" (6)." / "The memo stated that "[e]mployees must sign in" (Lopez 3)." / a 45-word block quotation introduced with a colon, indented, citation after the final period. ESL errors: period before the citation ("...sea." (6)); block quotations inside quotation marks; an ellipsis at the start of a quotation; inside/outside placement varying within one paper.
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 5 · transform 5 · classify 1 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.M.2 Commas, semicolons and colons in complex sentences (advanced control).** Rule: in a long sentence use commas to mark the clause boundaries the reader needs, semicolons to keep coordinate clauses apart when they already contain commas, and a colon only where the first part promises what the second delivers.
  - Must cover: comma before a sentential *which*; never a comma after a long compound subject; optional comma with *not only..., but also*; the *because* ambiguity rule (CMOS Q&A); the serial comma that prevents misreading ("my parents, Ayn Rand and God"); serial semicolons (MLA); a semicolon between clauses joined by *and* when they contain commas (Chicago 6.60); colon between two independent clauses (lowercase after — Chicago/MLA; capital — APA, accepted); commas with restrictive vs nonrestrictive participles; comma after *e.g.* and *i.e.* (US: comma; UK: often none — mark); *etc.* punctuation; *however* mid-sentence [VA24 11.LU.2].
  - Examples: "The board, which met twice, approved the budget; the staff, who had waited months, finally received back pay." / "The city had one option left: it had to sell the land." / "The study covered three groups: nurses, who worked nights; clerks, who worked days; and managers." ESL errors: comma chains (Chinese, Russian, Spanish); a missing serial comma that creates ambiguity; capitalisation after colons varying; *i.e.* for *e.g.* (all L1s).
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 5 · transform 5 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.M.3 Formatting to a style manual: italics vs quotation marks, headings, lists, abbreviations, foreign words.** Rule: a complete work is italicised, a part of a work goes in quotation marks, and every other formatting choice follows one manual consistently.
  - Must cover: italics for books, journals, films, newspapers, websites (MLA), ships, and foreign words on first use (Chicago; APA does not italicise common foreign words — mark); quotation marks for articles, chapters, poems, songs, episodes, web pages; headings (APA five levels; MLA flexible; Chicago headline style); vertical lists (MLA Style Center; Chicago); abbreviations defined at first use, *et al.*, *e.g.* only inside parentheses in APA — mark; Latin abbreviations not italicised; *Mr.* (US) / *Mr* (UK, accepted); *US* vs *U.S.* (Chicago accepts *US*; APA *U.S.* as adjective — mark); "Figure 1", "Table 2" capitalised.
  - Examples: "The article "Vaccines and Trust" appeared in *The Lancet*." / "Participants completed the survey (see Table 2)." / "Common biases (e.g., anchoring) distort budgets." ESL errors: underlining titles (older school habit); italics and quotation marks together; *etc.* after *e.g.*; "and etc."; quotation marks for emphasis (all L1s).
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 5 · transform 3 · classify 2 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.M.4 Capitalisation and spelling consistency across a long document.** Rule: decide each doubtful word once, record it on your own style sheet, and apply it the same way throughout the document.
  - Must cover: the personal style sheet (Chicago practice); proper adjectives (French, Victorian) vs lowercased eponyms (french fries, pasteurize, caesarean — Chicago/Merriam-Webster, mark variability); trademarks (Band-Aid; iPhone at a sentence start); open/hyphenated/closed compounds by dictionary (*health care / healthcare* — Merriam-Webster lists both, APA uses *health care*, mark); academic degrees (bachelor's degree; Master of Public Health); religions, historical periods (the Middle Ages, the 1960s), regions, kinship names; variants chosen once (judgment/judgement, toward/towards, gray/grey, adviser/advisor — Merriam-Webster: both); *email*, *website*, *internet* (Chicago 17, AP); search-and-replace consistency checks [CEFR-OC C1; VA24 9.LU.2.C].
  - Examples: "The plant used pasteurized milk and French cheeses." / "She earned a master's degree in public health, then a Master of Public Health from Emory." / "The report uses *healthcare* throughout; the appendix must match." ESL errors: capitalised generic nouns (German); lowercase proper adjectives (Romance L1s); mixed variants in one document (Commonwealth plus US schooling); over-capitalised job titles.
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 3 · error-spot 5 · transform 2 · classify 3 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **11.M.5 Punctuation for tone and rhythm; what to avoid in formal prose.** Rule: punctuation controls pace and emphasis — use the strong marks rarely so they keep their force, and never let punctuation do the work of a missing word.
  - Must cover: exclamation points almost never in academic or business prose; rhetorical question marks (10.M.1); no trailing ellipsis in formal writing; dashes for asides vs formal parentheses; semicolon rhythm; a colon for a reveal; scare quotes and *so-called*; no capitals for emphasis; never "?!"; one space after a period (Chicago, APA 7, MLA; two accepted in older practice — mark); consistent punctuation in bullet lists; *and/or* and slashes avoided; ampersand only in names and APA citations; emoji in workplace email (register).
  - Examples: "The council had a choice: raise taxes or cut services." / "The so-called reform cost more than it saved." / "Wait—was the invoice ever sent?" ESL errors: exclamation points in business email (Spanish, Portuguese, Russian, Arabic warmth norms); sentences ending in "..." (Chinese, Japanese, Korean chat habits); double spacing; slashes for *or* (all L1s); capitals for emphasis.
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 2 · error-spot 5 · transform 4 · classify 2 · next 1 · dictation 1.

Spiral: F and/then, sequencing, repetition [CEFR-CO A1; WIDA-LC L1] → B first/next; pronoun reference; demonstratives [CA App-B; CA PII.6.2a.Em] → C for example/however; synonyms and parallel expressions; tense consistency [CA App-B; WIDA-LC L3] → E given/new, paragraph openers, substitution/ellipsis, whole/part [WIDA-LC L4-L5] → M consequently/specifically; nominalisation as cohesion; audience-adapted organisation [CA App-B; WIDA-LC L6]. Paragraph and essay *structure* belong to Section 13; Section 12 owns the links between sentences and paragraphs. In this section `order` items may present whole sentences of a short text as the units to order.

### 12 · Foundations (A1) — 3 lessons, 45 exercises, bank 100

- **12.F.1 Linking a short text with and, then, after that.** Rule: to say what happens next, start the new sentence with *Then* or *After that*, or join two actions in one sentence with *and*.
  - Must cover: *and* inside a sentence vs *Then* opening the next one (capital, no comma after *Then* — Chicago; a comma after *After that* accepted either way); *and then*; time words as openers ("At 7, I..."); no more than two sentences in a row opening with *Then*; *Next* previewed (12.B.1).
  - Examples: "I get up at 6. Then I make coffee." / "She takes the bus and walks to the clinic." / "We eat lunch at noon. After that, we work until 5." ESL errors: *and* opening every sentence (Arabic *wa*); dropped subject after *Then* ("Then go to work" — Spanish, Portuguese, Chinese, Japanese); comma splices with *then* (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai); bare *After* as an adverb ("After, I go home" — French *après*, Spanish *después*; accepted informally, *After that* taught).
  - Exercises (15): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 3 · order 3 · match 1 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **12.F.2 Keeping the same topic: the name first, then he, she, it, they.** Rule: name the person or thing in the first sentence, then use *he, she, it* or *they* in the next sentences so the reader knows you mean the same one.
  - Must cover: name → pronoun chain over three or four sentences; return to the name when a second person appears; *it* for a thing or place; *they* for plurals; the possessive chain (Maria... her); the same subject across the text; never a dropped subject.
  - Examples: "Maria is a nurse. She works at night. Her hospital is in Dallas." / "My brothers live in Peru. They are farmers." / "This is my phone. It is new." ESL errors: dropped subject pronoun ("Is a nurse" — Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Arabic, Chinese, Japanese, Korean); he/she confusion (spoken Chinese, Turkish, Farsi, Tagalog, Swahili, Finnish, Hungarian); the name repeated in every sentence (Chinese, Japanese); *it* for people.
  - Exercises (15): mc 3 · click 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 2 · order 1 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **12.F.3 Adding and contrasting across sentences: also, too, but.** Rule: use *also* or *too* to add a similar idea in the next sentence, and *but* to add a different one.
  - Must cover: *too* at the end, *also* before the main verb or after *be*; *But* opening a sentence accepted in informal writing (Chicago) — the joiner inside the sentence is taught at this band; *and also* avoided; *not... either* previewed (10.B.5); one time frame across the text (*every day*, present tense).
  - Examples: "I like tea. I like coffee too." / "He works on Saturday. He also works on Sunday." / "The job is hard, but I like it." ESL errors: *also* at the end (accepted, less natural); *too* at the start; *but* with *although*; tense switching across the text (all L1s).
  - Exercises (15): mc 4 · click 2 · gap 3 · order 2 · match 1 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Foundations mix (the `order` item orders three sentences of a routine); bank 100 (34/33/33); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (8 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Cohesion & Text Connectives — Foundations Certificate.

### 12 · Building (A2) — 4 lessons, 64 exercises, bank 104

- **12.B.1 Ordering a process or a story: first, next, then, after that, finally; time phrases.** Rule: use one order word at the start of each step, with a comma after *First, Next* and *Finally*.
  - Must cover: *first/second/third* (*firstly* UK, accepted; Chicago prefers *first* — mark), *next, then* (no comma), *after that, later, finally*; *in the end* vs *at the end* vs *at last*; *at first* ≠ *first*; time phrases as organisers ("In the morning, ... At noon, ..."); dropping order words once the order is clear; past sequences with *when/after/before* (9.B.2) [CA App-B].
  - Examples: "First, wash your hands. Next, put on gloves. Finally, open the package." / "In 1903, the Wright brothers built a glider. Then they added an engine." / "At first the shop was quiet, but by noon it was full." ESL errors: *firstly... secondly... thirdly* throughout (Indian, Nigerian, British-schooled English — accepted); *at last* for *finally* in lists; bare *after*; *in the end* for *at the end* (Spanish *al final*, Portuguese).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 3 · error-spot 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · say-it 1.
- **12.B.2 Pronoun reference across sentences: clear antecedents, this/that/these/those, the + noun.** Rule: every pronoun must point to one clear noun in an earlier sentence; when two nouns could fit, repeat the noun or use *the* + noun.
  - Must cover: the ambiguous *she* ("Ana called Maria. She was angry."); *it* vs *this* (*this* = the whole previous idea); *the* + noun on second mention (a job → the job); *these/those*; *this* (near, recent) vs *that* (earlier); *they* for a company accepted informally, *it* in formal writing — mark; *one/ones* previewed [CA PII.6.2a.Em; WIDA-LC L2].
  - Examples: "I found a job at a bakery. The job starts Monday." / "The clinic gave us a schedule. This helped a lot." / "Ana called Maria because Maria was late." ESL errors: ambiguous pronouns (all L1s); *it* for *this* (Chinese); no article on second mention (Slavic, Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Turkish); *that* for *this* (Spanish *eso*).
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · click 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 2 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **12.B.3 Cause, result and purpose across sentences: so, that's why, for this reason, because of this, to + verb.** Rule: when the reason is in one sentence and the result in the next, start the result sentence with *So*, *That's why* or *For this reason*.
  - Must cover: *So* opening a sentence (neutral; comma optional); *That's why* + clause; *For this reason,* (formal); *because of this/that*; *as a result* previewed (12.C.3); purpose with *to* + verb / *in order to*; never *because* opening the result sentence; no chains of *so*.
  - Examples: "The bus was late. So I missed the first hour." / "Rent went up 20%. That's why we moved." / "I study at night to get a better job." ESL errors: *So that* for *So* (Chinese); *because* fragments; "for to" (Spanish, Portuguese); *that's why... because* doubled (Spanish *por eso... porque*); *therefore* in speech.
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · gap 4 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · transform 2 · match 1 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **12.B.4 One idea per paragraph: adding supports with also, another, one more.** Rule: put the main idea in the first sentence, add three to five sentences that support it, and use *also*, *another* or *one more* to add each new support.
  - Must cover: topic sentence → supports → closing sentence (structure itself is Section 13; here the links and the topic check); adding links (*also, another reason, one more thing*; *in addition* previewed); same subject and time frame throughout; *for example* previewed (12.C.1); deleting a sentence that changes the topic.
  - Examples: "My town has three problems. First, the roads are bad. Another problem is the water. One more problem is the noise." / "Working nights has one advantage: the pay. It is also quieter." ESL errors: the topic sentence delayed to the end (Chinese, Japanese, Arabic rhetorical traditions); topic switched mid-paragraph (all L1s); one-sentence paragraphs; *another* + plural ("another problems").
  - Exercises (16): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 3 · order 3 · error-spot 2 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · transform 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Building mix (the `order` item orders a four-sentence paragraph); bank 104 (26 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (10 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Cohesion & Text Connectives — Building Certificate.

### 12 · Connecting (B1) — 5 lessons, 90 exercises, bank 110

- **12.C.1 Examples and additions: for example, for instance, such as, in addition, moreover, furthermore, also.** Rule: introduce an example with *for example* (comma after it at the start of a sentence) or *such as* (inside the sentence), and add a point with *in addition* or *also*.
  - Must cover: *for example* first or mid-sentence with commas; *such as* + noun (no comma when it identifies); *e.g.* only inside parentheses; *in addition / moreover / furthermore* (formal) vs *also* (neutral); *besides* informal; *as well as* never sentence-initial; a semicolon or period before a transition, a comma after [CA App-B; TX ELPS-26 L4].
  - Examples: "Many small businesses failed in 2020. For example, half the restaurants on Main Street closed." / "The plan cuts costs. In addition, it shortens the wait." / "Vaccines such as the MMR shot are given in childhood." ESL errors: *For example,* + fragment (all L1s); *such as* + clause; "in addition of" (Spanish); *moreover* in casual email (Indian English *further*); *Also,* opening a sentence (accepted).
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · order 1 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.C.2 Contrast and concession across sentences: however, on the other hand, in contrast, nevertheless, still, even so.** Rule: start a contrasting sentence with *However,* or put *however* mid-sentence between commas; use *on the other hand* only when the first side has been stated.
  - Must cover: positions and punctuation of *however* (the splice trap — 9.C.2); *on the one hand... on the other hand*; *in contrast / by contrast* (not *on the contrary*, which denies a statement); *nevertheless / nonetheless* (concession); *still, even so, all the same*; *whereas/while* inside a sentence; *but* vs *however* register [CA App-B].
  - Examples: "The drug works well. However, it is expensive." / "The old bridge was cheap to build; on the other hand, it needed repairs every year." / "The store lost money in 2023. Nevertheless, it kept all its staff." ESL errors: "in the other hand" (Spanish); *on the contrary* for *in contrast* (Spanish, French, Italian *al contrario*); *however* as a conjunction with no punctuation; "but however"; *Although* opening a sentence without a main clause.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 4 · transform 3 · classify 2 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.C.3 Result, conclusion and summary: therefore, as a result, consequently, thus, in conclusion, to sum up, overall.** Rule: use *therefore* or *as a result* to open a sentence that follows from the one before, and *in conclusion* or *to sum up* only once, at the end.
  - Must cover: *therefore* (formal), *so* (neutral), *as a result, consequently*, *thus/hence* (formal, usually no comma after); *in conclusion* once; *to sum up / in short / overall*; *finally* ≠ *in conclusion*; *therefore* needs real causation; a summary sentence restates and adds nothing.
  - Examples: "The river flooded twice in 2022. As a result, the town moved its clinic uphill." / "Overall, the merger saved money but cost jobs." / "In conclusion, the evidence supports a later start time." ESL errors: *therefore* in speech for *so*; *in conclusion* mid-text; "as a conclusion"; *thus* in every sentence (Chinese, Hindi/Urdu academic habit); *at the end* for *finally*.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 2 · match 1 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.C.4 Synonyms, general nouns and parallel expressions.** Rule: refer to the same thing in different words — a synonym, a general noun (*the problem, this issue, the plan*) or a pronoun — so the reader can follow without meeting the same word ten times.
  - Must cover: lexical chains (the company → the firm → the business → it); general "shell" nouns (issue, problem, situation, approach, idea, decision, process, change) with *this/these*; parallel expressions for repeated actions (*cut costs* → *reduce spending*); when repetition is right (key technical terms — never vary *the patient*); *the former/the latter* previewed; *this* always with a noun when the reference could be unclear (writing-centre guidance) [WIDA-LC L3; CA PII.6.2a.Ex].
  - Examples: "The council approved a new bus route. The plan will start in May, and this change should cut commute times." / "Marie Curie discovered radium. The scientist later won a second Nobel Prize." / "The clinic raised its fees. This decision angered patients." ESL errors: heavy repetition (Chinese, Japanese, Arabic); synonym with the wrong register; pronoun chains that lose the referent; bare *this*.
  - Exercises (18): mc 3 · click 2 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 3 · classify 1 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.C.5 Tense and viewpoint consistency across a paragraph.** Rule: choose one time frame for the paragraph and stay in it; change tense only when the time really changes, and signal the change with a time word.
  - Must cover: narrative past with past perfect for earlier events; present for general truths and for describing a text or chart ("The chart shows"); shifts signalled by adverbials ("Today, ... In 1990, ..."); consistent person (no drift into *you/one/we*); consistent subject focus; reported-speech shifts (10.C.2) [VA24 8.LU.1.E; TEKS 7.10.D.ii].
  - Examples: "In 1928 Fleming noticed that mold had killed the bacteria. Today, penicillin saves millions of lives." / "The report shows that sales fell in March. It recommends a new pricing plan." / "First we checked the wiring; then we replaced the fuse." ESL errors: tense drift (Chinese, Vietnamese, Thai — no tense marking; Arabic; Spanish historical present); generic *you* in formal text (all L1s); person shift *we... one... you*.
  - Exercises (18): mc 2 · click 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 4 · transform 3 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Connecting mix (the classify item sorts connectives by function; one transform joins two sentences with a connective of the right register); bank 110 (22 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (12 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Cohesion & Text Connectives — Connecting Certificate.

### 12 · Expanding (B2) — 5 lessons, 95 exercises, bank 110

- **12.E.1 Given before new: the known-new contract.** Rule: start a sentence with information the reader already has and end it with the new information, so the end of one sentence becomes the start of the next.
  - Must cover: old-to-new order (UNC "Flow"; Yale; UMass); the passive and *there is / it is* as tools for moving known information to the front (Section 4); cleft sentences for end-focus (9.M.1); topic string (same subject) vs chain (last item → next subject); checking flow by reading only the sentence openers; the B2 "jumpiness" diagnosis [CEFR-CO B2; WIDA-LC L5].
  - Examples: "The city built a new bridge in 2019. The bridge cut travel time by half. This saving attracted two new employers." / "Penicillin was discovered by Alexander Fleming." (known: penicillin) vs "Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin." (known: Fleming). ESL errors: new information first (German, Slavic, Japanese, Korean word-order habits); every sentence opening with a new subject; forced passives.
  - Exercises (19): mc 2 · click 3 · gap 2 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · order 2 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.E.2 Paragraph openers and transitions between paragraphs.** Rule: open each paragraph with a sentence that looks back to the previous paragraph and forward to the new point.
  - Must cover: linking openers ("This shift...", "Such changes...", "A second factor is...", "Having examined X, ..."); *this/these* + summary noun as the strongest link; enumerating openers; question openers (rare); no "Another thing is..." fillers; headings replacing some transitions in reports; paragraph-level transitions (UNC, GMU, UW-Madison) [VA24 8.W.3.A; WIDA-LC L5].
  - Examples: "Cost is not the only problem. A second, less visible obstacle is staffing." / "These figures explain why the council acted. Its decision, however, created a new problem." / "Having described the causes, this report now turns to solutions." ESL errors: no paragraph links (all L1s); *Firstly/Secondly* as the only device; the thesis repeated in every opener; openers that look back but announce nothing.
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · order 2 · classify 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.E.3 Substitution and ellipsis: one/ones, do so, so/not, the same, such, the former/the latter.** Rule: replace a repeated word or phrase with a short stand-in, or leave it out when the reader can supply it.
  - Must cover: *one/ones*; *do so / do it / do that*; *so/not* after think/hope/be afraid; *the same*; *such (a)*; *the former/the latter* (two items only — Grammarly); *respectively*; ellipsis in coordination and after *than/as* (9.M.4); substitution across sentences ("Some clinics adopted the software. Others did not.") [WIDA-LC L4].
  - Examples: "The old machines were slow; the new ones are fast." / "The nurses asked for a raise, and the doctors did the same." / "Two options remain: sell or merge. The former is faster; the latter is safer." ESL errors: full repetition (Chinese, Japanese, Korean); *do so* in the wrong tense; *the latter* with three items; *one* for an uncountable ("I need advice. Do you have one?").
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 2 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.E.4 Whole-part and class-member links; signposting lists.** Rule: show how items belong together — a whole and its parts, a class and its members — with words like *consists of, including, respectively, the first/the second*, and keep the same order throughout the text.
  - Must cover: whole/part chains (the engine — its pistons — the crankshaft); class → members (vehicles: cars, buses); *namely, that is, including, consists of, comprises* (*comprised of* is established usage — Merriam-Webster — but *comprises / consists of* taught, mark); enumeration signposts ("three factors: ... The first... The second..."); *respectively* order-matching; consistent naming of parts; numbered lists and headings in reports [WIDA-LC L5].
  - Examples: "The device has three parts: a sensor, a pump, and a display. The sensor..." / "The plant employs 400 people, including 60 engineers." / "Sales in 2022 and 2023 were $2 million and $3 million, respectively." ESL errors: "comprised of" (all L1s; accepted); "consists in"; three items announced, four given; *namely* without its comma.
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 4 · classify 2 · match 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.E.5 Argument connectives: claim, evidence, counterclaim, rebuttal, hedging.** Rule: signal each move of an argument with the right connective — a claim (*arguably, it is clear that*), evidence (*according to, as X shows*), a counterclaim (*admittedly, some argue that*) and a rebuttal (*however, nevertheless, this overlooks*).
  - Must cover: evidence signals; concession and counterclaim (*admittedly, granted, while it is true that, opponents argue*); rebuttal (*however, yet, this argument ignores*); hedges (*to some extent, it seems, may, tends to*) vs boosters (*clearly, undoubtedly*); at most one connective per sentence and not in every sentence; *in my opinion* at most once in a formal essay; informal *plus, anyway* excluded [WIDA-LE Argue; WIDA-LC L5 hedging].
  - Examples: "Admittedly, a later start time costs businesses money. However, the evidence on accident rates is clear: fewer crashes follow." / "According to the 2023 audit, the program saved $4 million." / "Some argue that the tax hurts small firms. This view overlooks the exemption for firms under 20 staff." ESL errors: *In my opinion* everywhere (exam-trained Spanish, Arabic, Chinese writers); *on the contrary* as a rebuttal opener; a connective in every sentence (the WIDA "redundancy" profile); "Some people say" with no rebuttal.
  - Exercises (19): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · classify 3 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Expanding mix (the `next` item tests the known-new position; one transform is a paragraph-opener rewrite); bank 110 (22 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (12 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Cohesion & Text Connectives — Expanding Certificate.

### 12 · Mastery (C1) — 4 lessons, 80 exercises, bank 104

- **12.M.1 Formal connectives with exact logic: consequently, hence, thus, accordingly, specifically, in particular, notably, namely, that is, conversely, in turn, to this end, insofar as.** Rule: choose the connective that names the exact relation — result, specification, contrast, sequence or purpose — and use it only when that relation truly holds.
  - Must cover: the result set (*consequently, hence, thus, accordingly*) and comma behaviour (*thus/hence* often without a comma — Chicago); specification (*specifically, in particular, notably, namely, that is / i.e.*); contrast (*conversely, by contrast*); sequence and causal chains (*in turn, subsequently* — not *consequently*); purpose (*to this end, with this in mind*); *insofar as / inasmuch as*; the fallacy check (*therefore* without cause; *conversely* without a reversal); at most one formal connective per two sentences [CA App-B].
  - Examples: "Costs rose 30%; consequently, the council postponed the project." / "The plan targets one group in particular: workers over 55." / "Higher rates slow borrowing; borrowing, in turn, slows building." ESL errors: *subsequently* = consequently (Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese); "hence why"; *conversely* for *on the other hand*; *namely* for *for example*; connective overload (all L1s).
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 4 · classify 3 · match 1 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.M.2 Nominalisation as cohesion.** Rule: turn the action of one sentence into a noun that opens the next ("The company expanded rapidly. This expansion...") so the text moves from known to new without repeating the clause.
  - Must cover: verb → noun (expand → expansion, decide → decision, fail → failure) and adjective → noun (accurate → accuracy); *this/such* + nominalisation as a summary link; nominalisation to condense [CA PII.11-12.7.Br; Section 2]; the cost — agentless, abstract, wordy prose — and when to unpack a nominalisation back into a verb (writing-centre guidance); register (reports, abstracts).
  - Examples: "The town rebuilt its levee in 2010. The reconstruction cost $9 million." / "Prices fell sharply. This decline hurt small farms." / "Inspectors found cracks in the bridge. Their discovery closed it for a year." ESL errors: nominalising everything (Romance and Arabic academic styles); invented noun forms ("the expandation"); bare *this*; the agent lost where it matters ("mistakes were made").
  - Exercises (20): mc 2 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 7 · classify 2 · match 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.M.3 Thematic progression; cohesion versus coherence.** Rule: a text is coherent when its ideas are in a logical order and cohesive when its sentences are linked — connectives cannot repair a bad order, so fix the order first.
  - Must cover: theme and rheme; constant-theme pattern (one subject throughout, e.g. a biography), linear pattern (each rheme becomes the next theme, e.g. a process), split-rheme pattern (one sentence announces two ideas, each then developed); diagnosing a "jumpy" paragraph; reverse outlining; the connective-removal test (if the text still reads well, the order is right); over-cohesion [CEFR-CO C1; WIDA-LC 9-12 L6].
  - Examples: constant theme — "Ada Lovelace was born in 1815. She studied mathematics. She wrote the first algorithm." / linear — "The pump draws water. The water fills the tank. The tank feeds the turbine." / split — "Two factors drove the change: cost and safety. Cost... Safety..." ESL errors: *however/moreover* inserted to hide disorder (all L1s); circling paragraphs (Arabic, Spanish, Farsi rhetorical patterns); constant theme that loses its pronoun; a topic announced then abandoned.
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · gap 2 · error-spot 3 · transform 5 · order 3 · classify 2 · next 1 · dictation 1.
- **12.M.4 Audience-adapted organisation; register of connectives; signposting.** Rule: match the shape of the text and the formality of its links to the reader — a report signposts with headings and formal connectives, an email links plainly, and an opinion piece uses short sentences and few links.
  - Must cover: signposting and metadiscourse ("This report examines... The first section... Having established..., the discussion now turns to..."); the register ladder (*plus/also → in addition → furthermore; so → therefore → consequently; but → however → nevertheless*); headings and lists as cohesion; email and memo cohesion (one point per paragraph); persuasive prose (short sentences; the rhetorical question, 10.M.1); reader orientation (*as noted above, see Section 3*; *the aforementioned* avoided); sentence-length variety as rhythm; the formulaic-connective warning (a link in every sentence reads as template prose) [WIDA-LC 9-12 L6; VA24 11.LU].
  - Examples: report — "Section 2 sets out the costs; Section 3 evaluates the risks." / email — "Quick update: the vendor confirmed Friday. Also, we still need the signed form." / opinion — "The bridge is safe. The budget is not." ESL errors: formal connectives in friendly email (*furthermore, hence* — Indian, Nigerian, Arabic, Chinese school English); *the aforementioned*; "as I said before"; identical connective rhythm in every sentence.
  - Exercises (20): mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · transform 6 · classify 3 · match 1 · next 1.
- **Test:** 15 Qs, standard Mastery mix (the 5 transform items include one nominalisation link, one register rewrite and one reorder of a disordered paragraph); bank 104 (26 per lesson); pass 12/15; remediation per §0 (12 fresh items per missed lesson). **Certificate:** Cohesion & Text Connectives — Mastery Certificate.
