# Curriculum plan: grammar sections 1-7 (Sentence anatomy → Adjectives, adverbs & comparison)

Prepared 2026-09-05 for the ATTP English Platform. Aligned to `docs/standards-mapping.md` (Section C spiral, standards tags in brackets) and `docs/curriculum/00-platform-frame.md` (lesson loop, exercise catalogue, bank and test rules, checkpoints CP1 = sections 1-4, CP2 = sections 5-8).

## 0. Conventions used in every section

- **Bands** (student-facing, never grades): Foundations (A1), Building (A2), Connecting (B1), Expanding (B2), Mastery (C1). Every section below is taught at all five bands per the spiral table (Section C of the standards mapping); the band where the section is *introduced* is marked (I).
- **Lesson format**: `Lx Title` → Rule (one plain sentence a slow beginner can follow) → Cover (variants and exceptions that must be taught) → Ex (2-3 model sentences; adult topics: work, family, health, money, community, travel, general knowledge) → Errors (common ESL errors, first-language tendencies tagged where the literature supports it) → Plan (exercise counts by type). Every lesson screen has read-aloud; examples come before terminology.
- **Exercise types** (frame §5, only these names): mc, click, next, gap, order, error-spot, transform, match, classify, listen-choose, dictation, say-it. Each lesson has 8-20 items. `say-it` is practice-only (phone recognizer); it never appears in section tests. `listen-choose` and `dictation` are used heavily at Foundations/Building because many beginners (including elders) learn ear-first.
- **Section test** (frame §4): 15 questions drawn at random from the section bank for that band, new draw each attempt, pass 80% (12/15). Default type mix per band unless a cell says otherwise:
  - Foundations / Building: mc 4 · click 1 · gap 3 · order 2 · error-spot 2 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1.
  - Connecting: mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · order 1 · transform 2 · classify 1 · listen-choose 1 · dictation 1.
  - Expanding / Mastery: mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 3 · match-or-classify 1 · listen-choose 1.
- **Bank** (frame §6): 100-120 items per section per band, generated in proportion to lesson weight (rule: 4 lessons → 100 items, 5 → 110, 6+ → 120). Default composition: Foundations/Building ≈ mc 20% · gap 20% · order 12% · error-spot 10% · click 8% · next 6% · match/classify 8% · listen-choose 10% · dictation 6%; Connecting and above ≈ mc 15% · gap 18% · error-spot 20% · transform 18% · order 7% · click/classify/match 12% · listen-choose 5% · dictation 5%. Every item: one correct answer, provably wrong distractors, accepted variants listed, reading level inside the band, two independent review passes, teacher spot-check, in-app problem report.
- **Remediation** (frame §4.5): every bank item carries the tag of the lesson that teaches it; a failed test lists the missed lessons and re-opens them with a fresh exercise set before a new test. Each cell below also gives the default *type → lesson* map used when an item's tag is missing or ambiguous.
- **Certificate**: on passing, "<Section name>, <Band> level" with student name, date and verification code.
- **Where sources disagree** (frame §6): teach the standard American classroom convention, name the variant on the explain screen, and accept both in answer keys where both are correct. Each section lists its disagreements and the decision taken.
- **First-language tags** follow Swan & Smith, *Learner English* (2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2001; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/studies-in-second-language-acquisition/article/abs/learner-english-a-teachers-guide-to-interference-and-other-problems-michael-swan-and-bernard-smith-eds-new-york-cambridge-university-press-1987-pp-xv-265/B3FC4A69B69DF30D63CB8FFEA634FED3) and the ESL pages of the writing centres cited below. They describe *tendencies*, are shown only to teachers (never to students), and every error listed is also common across other backgrounds.

## Section 1 — Sentence anatomy & sentence types

Standards anchor: TEKS 2.11.D.i / VA24 2.LU.2 (I) → TEKS 3.11.D.i, VA24 3.LU.1 → TEKS 6.10.D.i, VA24 8.LU.2.A → CA PII.7.6.Br, TEKS E1.9.D.i → VA24 11.LU.1, CI-C1.

### 1 · Foundations (A1) (I) — 5 lessons · 70 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 What a sentence is.** Rule: a sentence names who or what (the subject) and says what they do or are (the verb); it starts with a capital letter and ends with a mark. Cover: subject = name, noun or pronoun; verb = action or am/is/are; one-word answers are not sentences; every written sentence needs both parts (no "She teacher", no "Is cold"); capital + . ? !.
  Ex: "Ana works at a hospital." / "My brothers are tall." / "It is cold today." Errors: missing *be* ("She nurse" — Russian, Arabic, Chinese, Turkish, Hebrew, Vietnamese, Malay/Indonesian); missing subject ("Is hot today" — Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Arabic, Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Turkish); no capital or end mark (learners from Arabic-, Chinese- and Thai-script backgrounds). Plan (14): click 4 · mc 3 · gap 2 · order 2 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- **L2 Statements, questions, commands.** Rule: a statement tells and ends with a period; a question asks and ends with a question mark; a command tells someone to do something and starts with the verb. Cover: questions put am/is/are or do/does first (recognition only; forms in Section 10); commands have no written subject ("you" is understood); *Please* and *Don't*; exclamation mark only for strong feeling (rare in adult writing); rising/falling intonation.
  Ex: "The bank opens at nine." / "Does the bank open at nine?" / "Open the window, please." Errors: statement order for questions ("You are ready?" — Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, Arabic, Chinese); commands with *you* ("You open the door"); over-use of "!". Plan (13): classify 3 · mc 3 · order 3 · listen-choose 2 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **L3 Every sentence shows its subject.** Rule: English always writes the subject, even when it is obvious; use *it* for weather, time and distance and *there is/are* to say something exists. Cover: pronoun subjects; dummy *it* ("It is raining", "It is 5 o'clock"); *there is/are* (agreement in Section 5); one subject only (no "My father he works").
  Ex: "It is Monday." / "There are two banks on this street." / "We live near the market." Errors: dropped subject (Romance languages, Arabic, Japanese, Korean, Chinese, Turkish, Farsi); double subject "My father he works" (Arabic, Chinese, Spanish); "Have a bank here" for "There is a bank" (Chinese 有, Spanish *hay*, French *il y a*). Plan (14): click 4 · gap 4 · error-spot 2 · order 2 · listen-choose 1 · say-it 1.
- **L4 Word order: subject, verb, object, place, time.** Rule: say the subject, then the verb, then the object; put place and time at the end (place before time). Cover: SVO is fixed; adverbs of place/time at the end; a time phrase can come first (comma optional after a short one); adjectives go before nouns (Section 7); no adverb between verb and object ("I like coffee very much", not "I like very much coffee").
  Ex: "Luis drives a taxi in Lima." / "I call my mother every Sunday." / "On Fridays we eat fish." Errors: verb-final order (Japanese, Korean, Turkish, Hindi/Urdu, Farsi, Bengali, Tamil); adverb between verb and object (French, Spanish, Italian, Arabic); topic-first sentences "My country, the weather is hot" (Chinese, Japanese, Korean); inversion after a fronted time word "Yesterday went I home" (German, Dutch, Scandinavian). Plan (15): order 6 · next 3 · mc 2 · error-spot 2 · dictation 1 · say-it 1.
- **L5 *Be* joins the subject to a name, a description or a place.** Rule: use *am/is/are* to say what someone is, how they are, or where they are. Cover: forms and contractions; be + noun ("a nurse"), be + adjective ("tired"), be + place ("at work"); age, hunger, thirst, fear use *be* ("I am 40", "I am hungry"); negative with *not*, never with *do*.
  Ex: "I'm a driver." / "The children are at school." / "She isn't tired." Errors: "I have 40 years", "I have hunger" (Spanish, French, Portuguese, Italian); "I am agree" (Spanish, French); missing *be* (as L1). Plan (14): gap 4 · mc 3 · click 2 · order 2 · listen-choose 2 · say-it 1.
- Test: 15 of bank 110, Foundations mix. Remediation map: mc/click → L1, L3, L5 · classify → L2 · gap → L3, L5 · order → L2, L4 · error-spot → L3, L4 · listen-choose → L1, L2 · dictation → L2, L4. Certificate: **"Sentence Anatomy and Sentence Types, Foundations level"**.

### 1 · Building (A2) — 5 lessons · 68 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 Simple sentences with two subjects or two verbs.** Rule: a simple sentence has one subject-verb pair, but the subject or the verb can be two things joined by *and*. Cover: compound subject ("Ana and Luis work"), compound verb ("I cook and clean"); no comma before *and* when the second verb has the same subject; objects and complements; it is still one clause.
  Ex: "My wife and I own a small shop." / "The nurse checked my blood pressure and wrote a note." / "Bread and milk are cheap here." Errors: comma before a compound verb ("I cook, and clean"); *and* chains ("and… and… and" — Arabic *wa*, Chinese); repeated pronoun "Ana and Luis they work" (Arabic, Spanish). Plan (13): click 4 · mc 3 · transform 3 · error-spot 2 · say-it 1.
- **L2 Compound sentences with *and, but, or, so* + comma.** Rule: join two complete sentences with a comma and a joining word: *and, but, or, so*. Cover: the seven coordinators (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so — *for/nor/yet* receptive at this band); comma before the coordinator when both sides are complete sentences (American classroom convention; short clauses often omit it — accept both when each clause is ≤ 4 words); *so* (result) vs *because* (reason, makes a dependent clause — Section 9); no comma when the second part has no subject.
  Ex: "I wanted to call, but my phone was dead." / "You can pay now, or you can pay next week." / "The clinic was closed, so we went home." Errors: comma splice without a conjunction ("I was tired, I went home" — Spanish, French, Italian, Portuguese, Russian, Chinese, Arabic); *so* used as *because*; starting with *But* wrongly "corrected" (it is acceptable). Plan (14): mc 3 · order 3 · transform 3 · gap 2 · error-spot 2 · dictation 1.
- **L3 Fragments: pieces that are not sentences.** Rule: if a group of words has no subject, no complete verb, or starts with a word like *because/when/if* and then stops, it is a fragment — attach it to a sentence or complete it. Cover: missing subject ("Went to the bank."), *-ing* alone ("Working at night."), subordinate fragments ("Because the bus was late."), "For example…" fragments; fixes (add subject/verb; join to the sentence before or after); fragments are normal in speech and text messages but not in formal writing.
  Ex: "Because the bus was late, I missed the interview." / "I work at night and study in the day." (was: "Working at night and studying in the day.") Errors: *because* fragments (universal); *-ing* as the only verb "He working now" (Chinese, Vietnamese, Arabic, Russian — *be* omission); subject-less clauses (Romance, Arabic, East Asian languages). Plan (14): classify 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 4 · mc 2 · listen-choose 1.
- **L4 Run-ons and comma splices.** Rule: two complete sentences cannot sit together with only a comma or with nothing between them; use a period, a comma + *and/but/so*, or a semicolon. Cover: fused sentence vs comma splice; the three fixes (semicolon receptive at this band); *then* is not a joining word ("I ate, then I slept" is a splice in formal writing; keys mark it as an error); transition words like *however* cannot join (full treatment Connecting L3).
  Ex: "The shop closes at six. We should hurry." / "The shop closes at six, so we should hurry." Errors: comma splices (Romance, Slavic, Chinese, Arabic backgrounds especially); *then*-splices; very long *and* sentences. Plan (14): error-spot 4 · transform 4 · mc 3 · classify 2 · dictation 1.
- **L5 Starting a sentence with a time or place phrase.** Rule: you can put a time or place phrase first; add a comma after it if it is longer than two or three words (after a short one the comma is optional). Cover: front vs end position; comma after a long introductory phrase (Purdue), optional after a short one ("In 2019 I moved" / "In 2019, I moved" — accept both); never a comma between subject and verb; varying openers across a short paragraph; sequence openers (*First, / Then, / After that,*).
  Ex: "After work, I pick up my daughter." / "In our village there is no bank." / "Next year, we will open a second shop." Errors: comma after the subject ("My brother, works" — Arabic, Chinese); inversion after the opener ("After work go I home" — German, Dutch); no comma after long openers. Plan (13): order 3 · next 3 · gap 2 · error-spot 2 · mc 2 · say-it 1.
- Test: 15 of bank 110, Building mix. Remediation map: mc → L1-L5 · click → L1 · order/gap → L2, L5 · transform → L2-L4 · classify → L3, L4 · error-spot → L1, L3, L4, L5 · listen-choose → L3 · dictation → L2, L4. Certificate: **"Sentence Anatomy and Sentence Types, Building level"**.

### 1 · Connecting (B1) — 5 lessons · 73 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 Independent and dependent clauses.** Rule: a clause has a subject and a verb; an independent clause can stand alone, and a dependent clause starts with a word like *because/although/when/if* and needs a main clause. Cover: subordinators by meaning — time (*when, while, after, before, until, as soon as, since*), reason (*because, since, as*), contrast (*although, even though, though, whereas, while*), condition (*if, unless*), purpose (*so that*); relative clauses are dependent too (Section 3); *so* vs *so that*.
  Ex: "Although the factory closed, most workers found new jobs." / "We will start when everyone arrives." / "She studies at night because she works during the day." Errors: subordinator + coordinator together ("Although he was ill, but he came" — Chinese, Arabic, Farsi, Turkish, Korean, Japanese, Malay); *even* for *even though* (Spanish *aunque*); *because* fragments. Plan (15): click 4 · classify 4 · mc 3 · gap 2 · error-spot 2.
- **L2 Building complex sentences and placing the comma.** Rule: if the dependent clause comes first, put a comma after it; if it comes second, usually no comma. Cover: both orders; contrast clauses (*although, whereas, while*) often take a comma even in second position (accept both); *because* after a negative main clause can be ambiguous ("I didn't leave because I was tired"); the dependent clause keeps its own subject; commas with relative clauses only when non-defining (Section 3).
  Ex: "If the price drops, we will buy more stock." / "We will buy more stock if the price drops." / "The nurses kept working, although the power was out." Errors: comma before every dependent clause ("I stayed home, because it rained" — Russian, Polish, German, where the comma is obligatory); missing comma after a long fronted clause; verb-final order inside the clause ("…because he tired was" — German, Dutch, Turkish). Plan (15): transform 4 · order 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 3 · mc 2.
- **L3 Comma splices with transition words.** Rule: words like *however, therefore, then, also* and *for example* are not joining words; between two sentences use a period or a semicolon, then a comma after the word. Cover: conjunctive adverbs (*however, therefore, moreover, otherwise, meanwhile, consequently, then, also, for example, in addition*); patterns ". However," and "; however,"; mid-sentence *however* between commas; the three families (coordinators, subordinators, conjunctive adverbs); semicolon between closely related sentences.
  Ex: "The shop was busy; however, the staff stayed calm." / "The price rose. Therefore, we changed supplier." / "We finished early, so we went for coffee." Errors: the *however* splice (universal, very frequent in Spanish-, Arabic- and Chinese-background academic writing); semicolon used as a colon or comma (Greek, Arabic — Greek "؛/;" transfer); *so that* for *so*. Plan (14): error-spot 4 · transform 3 · mc 3 · gap 2 · classify 2.
- **L4 Fragments at paragraph level.** Rule: check every sentence in a paragraph — a piece starting with *which/who/because/although*, an *-ing* phrase, an example ("Such as…") or a list is not a sentence by itself. Cover: subordinate-clause fragments; relative fragments ("Which is why…"); appositive, *-ing* and *to*-phrase fragments ("To save money."); "for example / such as" fragments; fixes (attach with a comma or dash, or add subject + verb); intentional fragments in ads and social posts vs formal writing (Grammarly, UNC) — keys treat fragments as errors in formal-register items only.
  Ex: "We chose the cheaper plan, which covers dental care." / "To save money, we cook at home." Errors: as Building L3 plus *Which…* fragments (universal) and list fragments after a colon. Plan (14): classify 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 4 · mc 2 · dictation 1.
- **L5 Combining short sentences and cutting stringy ones.** Rule: join short choppy sentences with a subordinator or a relative pronoun; break a long sentence that keeps using *and/so/then* into two or three. Cover: sentence combining by embedding (relative clause, appositive, *-ing* phrase — preview of Expanding L4); choosing the subordinator that shows the relationship (cause, time, contrast); stringy "and… and… so…" sentences; one idea per sentence in informational paragraphs; varied openers.
  Ex: "Marie Curie was born in Warsaw. She won two Nobel Prizes." → "Marie Curie, who was born in Warsaw, won two Nobel Prizes." / "After the plane landed, we collected our bags and took a taxi." (was a four-clause *and* chain). Errors: over-long *and/wa* chains (Arabic, Chinese, Spanish speech-style writing); over-short choppy sentences (Japanese, Korean); *which* for people. Plan (15): transform 6 · order 3 · mc 2 · error-spot 2 · match 2.
- Test: 15 of bank 110, Connecting mix. Remediation map: mc → L1-L5 · click/classify → L1, L3, L4 · gap → L1-L3 · order → L2, L5 · transform → L2-L5 · error-spot → L1-L5 · listen-choose → L1 · dictation → L4 · match → L5. Certificate: **"Sentence Anatomy and Sentence Types, Connecting level"**.

### 1 · Expanding (B2) — 5 lessons · 72 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 Compound-complex sentences.** Rule: a compound-complex sentence has at least two independent clauses and at least one dependent clause; punctuate each join by its own rule. Cover: identifying every clause; comma + coordinator between independents; comma after a fronted dependent clause, none before a trailing one; keep to three clauses in most academic prose; parallel form in coordinated clauses (Section 9).
  Ex: "Although the vaccine was new, doctors trusted it, and patients lined up for it." / "We repaired the roof because the rains were coming, but the walls still leaked." Errors: a comma at every clause boundary (Russian, German, Polish); missing comma between independents; *although… but*; run-ons. Plan (15): click 4 · classify 3 · transform 4 · error-spot 2 · mc 2.
- **L2 Semicolons, colons and dashes between clauses.** Rule: a semicolon joins two closely related complete sentences; a colon introduces an explanation or list after a complete sentence; a dash sets off an interruption or a dramatic addition. Cover: semicolon + conjunctive adverb; semicolons in lists with internal commas; colon only after a complete clause (never after *such as / including*); capital after a colon (Chicago: only if two or more sentences follow; APA: capital when a full sentence follows — accept both); em dash closed (Chicago/APA) vs spaced en dash (BrE) — accept both; parentheses for asides.
  Ex: "The test has two parts: a written paper and an interview." / "Two inventors claimed the telephone; only one got the patent." / "The plan — if we can call it that — failed in a week." Errors: semicolon before a dependent clause; colon after *such as*; dash over-use; punctuation transfer (Greek, Arabic). Plan (14): mc 4 · gap 3 · error-spot 4 · transform 2 · dictation 1.
- **L3 Controlled variety: openers, length and rhythm.** Rule: vary how sentences start and how long they are on purpose — a short sentence after two long ones makes a point land. Cover: openers (prepositional phrase, *-ing/-ed* phrase, dependent clause, adverb, appositive); alternating length; keep subject and verb close (no more than ~8 words apart); avoid "The… The… The…" starts; active/passive choice for focus (Section 4).
  Ex: "Built in 1889, the tower was meant to last 20 years. It is still there." / "Every morning, before the market opened, the fishermen sold their catch on the quay." Errors: uniform SVO openings (Chinese, Japanese, Korean writers); stacked-clause sentences (Arabic, Spanish, German academic style); fronted clauses without commas. Plan (14): order 3 · transform 5 · mc 3 · classify 2 · next 1.
- **L4 Appositives, participial phrases and dangling modifiers.** Rule: a describing phrase must sit next to the word it describes, and an opening *-ing/-ed* phrase must describe the subject of the sentence. Cover: appositives with commas (non-essential) or without (essential: "my brother Luis"); present and past participial phrases; absolute phrases ("Its engine failing, the plane turned back"); dangling and misplaced modifiers (*only, almost*); fixes (name the doer; rewrite as a clause).
  Ex: "Alexander Fleming, a Scottish doctor, discovered penicillin in 1928." / "Working late, the nurses finished the reports." (correct) vs "Working late, the reports were finished." (dangling). Errors: dangling participles (universal; heavier for subject-drop languages); missing commas around non-essential appositives; *which* used as an appositive. Plan (15): error-spot 4 · transform 4 · mc 3 · click 2 · order 2.
- **L5 Emphasis: clefts, fronting and end-focus.** Rule: to stress one part of a sentence, move it — "It was the price that stopped us" or "What stopped us was the price". Cover: it-clefts; wh-clefts; "the reason (why)… is that" (Merriam-Webster records "is because" as common; style guides prefer "is that" — teach *that*, accept *because* only in informal items); fronting for contrast ("This one I like"); end-weight (long, new information last); presentational *there*.
  Ex: "It was in 1969 that people first walked on the Moon." / "What the clinic needs is a second nurse." / "The reason we moved is that rents doubled." Errors: agreement in clefts ("It is the workers who is…"); "What… is because"; cleft over-use. Plan (14): transform 5 · mc 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 2 · listen-choose 1.
- Test: 15 of bank 110, Expanding mix. Remediation map: mc → L1-L5 · click → L1, L4 · classify → L1, L3 · gap → L2, L5 · order → L3, L4 · transform → L1, L3-L5 · error-spot → L1, L2, L4, L5 · listen-choose → L5 · dictation → L2 · next → L3. Certificate: **"Sentence Anatomy and Sentence Types, Expanding level"**.

### 1 · Mastery (C1) — 5 lessons · 72 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 Embedded clauses as subjects and objects.** Rule: a whole clause can be the subject or object of a sentence — "That the cure worked surprised everyone"; "Nobody knew whether it would work". Cover: *that*-clauses (*that* optional after verbs of saying/thinking, kept after nouns and in subject position); *wh*-clauses (*what/whether/how*); extraposition with *it* ("It surprised everyone that…"); "the fact that…"; clause as complement ("The problem is that…"); embedded questions keep statement order (Section 10); no comma before a *that*-clause; clause subjects take a singular verb (Section 5).
  Ex: "That most volunteers stayed for a second year says a lot about the program." / "What the audit revealed was a pattern of late payments." / "It is unclear whether the bridge will reopen." Errors: question order inside embedded questions ("I don't know what is the answer" — Romance, Arabic, Chinese, universal); missing *it* ("Is clear that…" — Spanish, Portuguese, Italian, Arabic); "the fact that" over-use. Plan (14): click 3 · transform 4 · mc 3 · error-spot 2 · gap 2.
- **L2 Verbals for concision: participle, gerund and infinitive phrases.** Rule: turn a clause into a verbal phrase when the subject is the same — "After she finished the audit, she…" → "Having finished the audit, she…". Cover: present, past and perfect participle phrases; reduced relative clauses; gerund phrases as subjects/objects, with possessive ("Ana's arriving late" formal; "Ana arriving late" informal — accept both); infinitive of purpose; dangling and squinting modifiers; punctuation of sentence-initial verbal phrases; over-reduction that hides the agent.
  Ex: "Having lost the contract, the firm cut prices." / "Elected in a landslide, the mayor promised new housing." / "To reduce waste, the hospital switched to reusable gowns." Errors: dangling verbals; aspect mismatch ("Finishing the audit last year, she…" for "Having finished"); "Being that…"; gerund/infinitive choice after verbs (Section 4). Plan (15): transform 6 · error-spot 4 · mc 3 · order 2.
- **L3 Inversion and fronting for emphasis.** Rule: after a negative or restrictive opener (*never, rarely, not only, only then, under no circumstances*), put the auxiliary before the subject. Cover: negative-adverbial inversion (*never, seldom, rarely, hardly… when, no sooner… than, not only… but also, only after/when/if, little, under no circumstances, on no account, at no time*); "so/such… that" inversion ("So severe was the drought that…"); *nor/neither/so do I*; conditional inversion (*had/were/should* — Section 6); fronted place + verb of position ("On the hill stands a chapel"); *do*-support with present simple ("Rarely does the committee…"); when inversion sounds pompous.
  Ex: "Not only did the dam hold, but it also powered the whole valley." / "Never before had so many people voted." / "Only after the fire did the council act." Errors: inversion after non-negative openers ("Yesterday did I go" — German, Dutch V2 transfer); no *do*-support ("Rarely the committee meets"); double negation with *never… not*. Plan (15): transform 6 · order 3 · error-spot 3 · mc 2 · listen-choose 1.
- **L4 Periodic and cumulative sentences, end-focus and rhythm.** Rule: a periodic sentence saves the main point for the end; a cumulative sentence states it first and adds detail; use both, and keep the subject and verb close. Cover: periodic vs cumulative; end-weight and given-new order (Section 12); parallelism across clauses (Section 9); commas/dashes with cumulative modifiers; typical lengths by genre (news ≈ 20 words; academic 25-35); reading aloud to test rhythm; intentional fragments in journalism/marketing vs academic prohibition.
  Ex: "After the floods, the strikes and the audit, the company finally admitted its mistake." (periodic) / "The company admitted its mistake — slowly, reluctantly, and only after the audit." (cumulative). Errors: front-loading every sentence (German academic transfer); left-branching stacks that delay the verb by 20+ words (Japanese, Korean); "rhythmic" comma splices (Romance). Plan (13): classify 3 · transform 4 · order 3 · mc 2 · say-it 1.
- **L5 Editing sentences for concision and clarity.** Rule: cut words that add nothing — turn *there is / it is* openers, nominalized verbs and doubled phrases into strong subjects and verbs. Cover: expletive openers ("There are many people who…" → "Many people…"); nominalizations back to verbs (Section 2 Mastery); redundancy ("past history", "end result"); stacked prepositional phrases; reduced relatives; passive → active where the agent matters (Section 4); *that* vs *which* (Chicago/APA/Purdue: *that* restrictive, *which* + commas non-restrictive; Cambridge/BrE allow restrictive *which* — teach the American convention, accept unambiguous *which*).
  Ex: "There are three reasons that explain why the bridge failed." → "The bridge failed for three reasons." / "The implementation of the policy by the council was slow." → "The council implemented the policy slowly." Errors: wordy transfer from rhetorical traditions (Arabic, Spanish, Italian; French *il y a*); "the… of the… of" chains; restrictive *which* without commas from BrE-trained learners (accepted, flagged). Plan (15): transform 7 · error-spot 4 · mc 2 · click 2.
- Test: 15 of bank 110, Mastery mix. Remediation map: mc → L1-L5 · click → L1, L5 · classify → L4 · gap → L1 · order → L2-L4 · transform → L1-L5 · error-spot → L1-L3, L5 · listen-choose → L3. Certificate: **"Sentence Anatomy and Sentence Types, Mastery level"**.

Errors: over-variation (Arabic, Spanish, French rhetorical tradition); pronoun chains with no noun for a whole paragraph (Chinese, Japanese null-subject transfer); *it* with competing antecedents. Plan (14): click 4 · transform 4 · mc 3 · error-spot 2 · match 1.
- **L2 Demonstratives and summarising nouns.** Rule: after stating an idea, point back to it with *this/these/such* + a summarising noun (this approach, these findings, such measures) rather than a bare *this*. Cover: *this/that/these/those* with "shell nouns" (approach, issue, finding, claim, process, situation, trend, measure, view); bare *this* as a whole-idea pronoun (accepted but weaker — Purdue/Walden: add a noun); *that/those* for distance or contrast; *such* (+ *a/an* with singular countables); *the above/the following* (formal), *the said* (legal); *the former/the latter* (two items only).
  Ex: "Rents doubled in three years. This increase pushed nurses out of the city." / "Such measures rarely work without enforcement." / "The report lists two options; the latter is cheaper." Errors: bare *this* chains; "this kind of measures" (number mismatch — Romance, Chinese); *the former* with three items; "such a measures". Plan (14): gap 5 · transform 4 · mc 3 · error-spot 2.
- **L3 Forward reference, ellipsis and comparative reference.** Rule: a pronoun may come before its noun inside the same sentence ("Although she was tired, Ana finished"), and words like *respectively, the other, another, the same* point sideways or back — keep every link recoverable. Cover: cataphora within a sentence; *respectively*; *one… the other / another / the others*; *the same… as*, *similar/different/such*; ellipsis in parallel clauses ("Some prefer tea; others, coffee"); *so/nor* inversion in ellipsis; *do so* in academic style; pronoun-free rewriting to avoid gender assumptions (APA).
  Ex: "Although it began as a hobby, her repair shop now employs six people." / "Ana and Luis were born in 1980 and 1985 respectively." / "One brother stayed in Kabul; the other moved to Hamburg." Errors: cataphora across sentences ("She was tired. Ana went home."); *respectively* misordered; "the other ones" for uncountables; "another people" (Spanish *otra gente*). Plan (15): transform 5 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · mc 2 · order 1.
- **L4 Advanced relatives and free relatives.** Rule: use *what*-clauses as subjects ("What matters is cost"), *whoever/whatever* for "any person/thing that", and formal links like *whereby, in which case, at which point, of which* to keep long sentences clear. Cover: nominal relatives as subject/object; *whereby* (by means of which), *wherein/whereupon* (formal/legal), *in which case, at which point, by which time, the extent to which, the degree to which*; preposition + *which/whom* in formal writing; one relative clause per noun; no "…which… which…" chains.
  Ex: "The council introduced a scheme whereby tenants can buy their flats." / "Prices may fall, in which case we will buy." / "The extent to which diet affects mood is unclear." Errors: *what* for *which* in sentence relatives; "the way how"; *in which case* used as *in that case*; *whereby* as *where*. Plan (15): transform 5 · gap 4 · mc 3 · error-spot 3.
- **L5 Impersonal and generic reference; register control.** Rule: choose the reference style the genre expects — *one* or a general noun in formal essays, *we* for the writer's team, an agentless passive when the agent is unknown or irrelevant, and *I/we* for the author in APA style. Cover: *one/one's* (formal, consistent — no *one… his*); generic *you* (informal; avoided in essays); *we* inclusive/exclusive; "the author" (older APA) vs *I/we* (APA 7 recommends first person); *they* generic and non-binary (M-W, APA 7); "it is argued / it can be seen" and passives as reference strategies; consistency within a document.
  Ex: "One cannot judge a policy by its first year." / "In this study we compared two clinics." / "It is often argued that remote work lowers costs." Errors: "one… his/you" shifts; "the author" and first person in the same text; singular *they* rejected as "plural only" by traditionally trained learners (accepted); generic *you* in formal items (error). Plan (14): mc 4 · classify 3 · transform 4 · error-spot 3.

### 4 · Foundations (A1) (I) — 5 lessons · 70 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 *Be* in the present.** Rule: use *am* with *I*, *is* with *he/she/it* and one person or thing, *are* with *you/we/they* and more than one. Cover: full and short forms (*'m, 's, 're*); negatives (*isn't, aren't, I'm not* — there is no "amn't"); yes/no questions by moving *be* first; short answers ("Yes, I am." — never contracted when positive); *be* for name, job, age, feeling, place.

- **L2 Present simple: habits, facts, timetables.** Rule: for things that are always true or happen regularly use the base verb; with *he, she, it* add *-s*. - **L3 Present continuous: now and around now.** Rule: for an action happening now or these days, use *am/is/are* + verb-*ing*. - **L4 Tense and voice consistency in essays and reports.** Rule: keep tense and voice steady inside a paragraph, follow the tense conventions of the genre, and switch only for a reason the reader can see. - **L3 Correlatives and paired subjects in formal writing.** Rule: *both… and* takes a plural verb; *not only… but also, either… or* and *neither… nor* agree with the nearer subject; when that sounds awkward, rewrite. Cover: *both… and* → plural; *not only… but (also)* → nearer; *either/neither… or/nor* → nearer; *or* with singulars → singular; rewriting awkward proximity ("Neither the nurses nor the doctor was" → "The doctor was not…, and neither were the nurses"); *as well as* is not a coordinator.
  Ex: "Both the landlord and the tenant have signed." / "Not only the players but also the coach was fined." / "Either the printers or the router is faulty." Errors: plural by default with *either… or*; *as well as* treated as *and*; "not only… but also… were" with a singular nearer subject. Plan (14): gap 5 · mc 4 · error-spot 3 · transform 2.
- **L4 Relative clauses, *what*-clauses and sentence-relative *which*.** Rule: *who/which/that* takes the number of the noun it refers to; a *what*-clause subject usually takes a singular verb; a *which* that refers to a whole clause is singular. Cover: "one of the few countries that have…" (plural) vs "the only one of… who has" (singular); *which* referring to a clause ("Prices fell, which was good"); *what*-clause + singular verb, plural acceptable when the complement is plural ("What we need is/are more nurses" — Cambridge, M-W: both) → accept both; *there* + compound subject; long "the… who… are…" chains.
  Ex: "Nepal is one of the few countries that have never been colonised." / "What worries me is the cost." / "What the clinic needs are volunteers." (also: is) Errors: "one of the few countries that has" (accepted, flagged); "which were" for a clause; "what we need are money". Plan (14): mc 5 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · classify 2.
- **L5 Notional agreement and register.** Rule: some subjects agree by meaning rather than form — *a couple of nurses are*, *the public is/are*, *none is/are* — choose the form the genre expects and keep it consistent. Cover: notional agreement; *a couple of* + plural → plural; *number/percentage/proportion of*; *the public, the audience, the government* (AmE singular, BrE plural); *none* (M-W both); company and band names ("The Beatles were"; "Coldplay is/are" — BrE plural); *politics/statistics* (field singular, facts plural); *each* after a plural subject; consistency within one document; style-manual notes (APA: *data* plural; Chicago: collective nouns by sense).
  Ex: "A couple of nurses are off sick." / "The public is entitled to see the report." / "Statistics is hard, but these statistics are clear." Errors: register clash ("the team are" in an AmE-style report); *none* over-corrected to singular; "a couple of nurses is"; "statistics are hard". Plan (14): classify 4 · gap 4 · mc 3 · error-spot 3.

### 5 · Mastery (C1) — 5 lessons · 71 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 Nominalized subjects.** Rule: in a nominalized subject the abstract head noun controls the verb — "The implementation of the rules has…", "Delays in payment are…" — and two abstract nouns joined by *and* are plural unless they name one concept. Cover: head vs *of*-phrase; abstract heads with plural complements; *and*-linked abstractions as one idea ("Trial and error is"; "Research and development has/have" — notional → accept both when one concept); *-ing* nominalizations ("The testing of samples takes"); "the failure of… to…"; cross-reference Section 2 Mastery L2.
  Ex: "The introduction of two new shifts has reduced overtime." / "Reductions in funding across all departments are expected." / "Law and order is a campaign issue." - **L2 Abstract and academic nouns: false plurals and Latin plurals.** Rule: *evidence, feedback, research* and *information* are singular however much you mean; *data, criteria, phenomena* and *media* are plural forms — follow your style manual and stay consistent. Cover: uncountable academic nouns + singular verb; *data* (M-W: singular or plural; APA and scientific style plural) → plural in science items, either elsewhere; *criteria/phenomena/bacteria* plural; *media* singular in everyday use; *series/species* invariable; *-ics* nouns; *a piece of evidence is*; *a range/variety of* + plural → plural (usually).
  Ex: "The evidence suggests a link between shift work and heart disease." / "These criteria are applied to every application." / "The media has/have reported the story." Errors: "evidences suggest", "the informations are"; "this criteria is"; "the data is" in a science item (accepted elsewhere); "a range of options is available" (accepted; plural more common). - **L3 Clause, quantity and statistical subjects.** Rule: a *that*-clause or *whether*-clause subject is singular; *a total of / an average of / x percent of* agree with the noun after *of*; *the total / the average* is singular. - **L4 Distance, parentheticals and elliptical subjects.** Rule: commas, dashes and phrases like *as well as* do not change the number of the subject; when two things share one noun, the noun's number decides. - **L5 Consistency editing at Mastery.** Rule: read every long sentence backwards from the verb to the head noun; agreement errors hide where the subject is far from the verb, inverted, or notional. Cover: attraction errors ("The cause of these problems are"); proximity traps in inverted and existential sentences; notional choices (collective nouns, *none*, *data*) fixed once in a style sheet and applied throughout; pronoun agreement that follows verb agreement ("the committee… it/its"); checking *of*-phrases before verbs across a paragraph.
  Ex: "The cause of these problems is poor training." / "There are, in most districts, a clinic and a pharmacy." / "The team has published its findings; its members are now touring." Errors: as above; mixed "team is… they" chains; plural pronoun after "there is a clinic and a pharmacy". Plan (14): error-spot 6 · transform 4 · mc 2 · click 2.

### 6 · Expanding (B2) — 6 lessons · 86 exercises · bank 120
- **L1 Speculation and deduction across time.** Rule: match the modal to how sure you are and the form to the time — *must/can't/may/might/could* + *be* (now), + *have* + past participle (past), *will/should* + *be* (assumption about the present or future).   Ex: "The workers must have left before the storm hit." / "Someone's at the door — that'll be the plumber." / "The results should be ready by noon." Errors: *can* for a specific present possibility ("He can be at home" — Romance, German); "must have to"; *should have arrived* for an expectation; *will be* for deduction about the past. Plan (15): gap 5 · mc 4 · transform 3 · error-spot 3.
- **L2 *Needn't have, didn't need to, could have, was able to*.** Rule: *needn't have done* means you did it but it was unnecessary; *didn't need to do* means it was unnecessary (and usually you didn't do it); *could have done* means it was possible but did not happen. Cover: the contrast (Cambridge, British Council); *should have* vs *ought to have*; *could have* (unused ability) vs *was able to / managed to* (achieved); *would have liked to*; *had to* vs *must have* (obligation vs deduction).
  Ex: "You needn't have cooked; we brought dinner." / "We didn't need to book, so we just turned up." / "She could have become a surgeon, but she chose nursing." - **L3 Mixed conditionals.** Rule: mix the times when the condition and the result happen at different times — "If I had studied (past), I would have a better job now (present)"; "If I were richer (now), I would have bought it (past)". Cover: both mixes; *were* for all persons in formal style; *otherwise* as a condition substitute; *but for / without / if it weren't for / if it hadn't been for*; punctuation; explaining present situations by past causes (work and health narratives).
  Ex: "If the council had repaired the bridge, we wouldn't be taking the long road today." / "If she weren't so careful with money, she would have lost the house years ago." Errors: mechanical matching (third + third for a present result); *would* in the *if*-clause; "if it wasn't for" (accepted informal); "if the council would have". Plan (14): gap 5 · transform 4 · mc 3 · error-spot 2.
- **L4 Hedging and stance in academic and professional writing.** Rule: use modals and adverbs to show how sure you are — *may/might/could* for possibility, *should/would* for expectation, *must/will* for certainty — and match the strength of the claim to the evidence. Cover: hedges (*may, might, could, would seem/appear, tend to, is likely to, possibly, arguably, to some extent*); boosters (*must, clearly, certainly, undoubtedly*); recommendation strength (*could* < *should* < *must*); no stacking ("it may possibly perhaps"); WIDA-LC L5 "modal verbs, hedging, evaluative expressions"; hedging in emails ("I would suggest", "It might be worth").
  Ex: "Shift work may increase the risk of heart disease." / "The findings would seem to support the earlier study." / "Managers should consider shorter shifts." Errors: absolute claims on thin evidence (universal); *must* for recommendations (Arabic, Chinese, Russian directness); *can* as a hedge for a specific case ("This can be the reason"); *would* over-use. - **L5 Alternatives to *if*: *unless, provided, as long as, in case, even if, whether*.** Rule: *unless* = if not; *provided that / as long as* = only if; *in case* = as a precaution; *even if* = it makes no difference; *whether… or not* = in either case. Cover: *unless* (no double negative); *provided (that) / providing (that) / on condition that*; *as long as / so long as*; *in case* (precaution, present tense; vs *if*); *even if / even though* (contrast); *whether… or not*; *suppose / supposing*; *given that* (Mastery); punctuation with fronted clauses.
  Ex: "You can't work night shifts unless you have a medical certificate." / "Take an umbrella in case it rains." / "Even if the rent falls, we won't move back." Errors: "unless… not"; *in case* used as *if* ("In case it rains, we stay" — Spanish *en caso de que*, French *au cas où*); *even* alone for *even if*; "whether if". Plan (14): gap 5 · mc 4 · transform 3 · error-spot 2.
- **L6 Politeness, requests and formal modality.** Rule: the further a modal moves into its past form (*can → could, will → would, may → might*), the more polite or tentative it sounds. Cover: the request ladder (*Can you / Could you / Would you / Would you mind + -ing / I was wondering if you could*); *May I* (formal); *shall* in offers and in legal or contract language ("The tenant shall pay") — M-W: *shall* rare outside these uses; softening refusals ("I'm afraid I can't"); *would* for tentative opinions ("I would argue"); *might* for tentative suggestions ("You might want to…").
  Ex: "Would you mind sending the form again?" / "I was wondering if you could cover my shift on Friday." / "The contractor shall complete the work by 30 June." Errors: "Would you mind to send"; "I was wondering if you can" (accepted informal); over-formal *shall* in speech; *please* as the only softener (Arabic, Chinese, Russian directness transfer). ### 6 · Mastery (C1) — 5 lessons · 71 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 Past modals in narrative and argument.** Rule: use modal + *have* + past participle to reason about the past — what must have happened, what could have gone differently, what should have been done — including passive and continuous forms. Cover: counterfactual history ("Had the vaccine failed, millions might have died"); *would have been / could have been + -ing*; passive past modals ("should have been checked"); *was/were to have* + participle; *needn't have*; *ought to have*; modal + *have* in reported speech and formal reports; negatives ("might not have been").
  Ex: "The fire could have been prevented if the alarms had been tested." / "The archive must have been damaged before 1950, because no later records survive." / "The bridge was to have been finished by spring." - **L2 Inverted conditionals.** Rule: in formal writing you can drop *if* and move *had, were* or *should* to the front — "Had I known…", "Were it not for…", "Should you need…".   Ex: "Had the council acted sooner, the flooding would have been minor." / "Were it not for her scholarship, she could not have studied." / "Should you require further information, please contact us." - **L3 Implied and reduced conditions.** Rule: a condition can hide inside a word or phrase — *otherwise, if so, if any, if necessary, with/without, but for, given that, assuming that* — so read the sentence for the condition it implies. Cover: *otherwise* (= if not); *if so / if not / if any / if necessary / if in doubt / if ever*; *with / without* + noun ("Without insurance, we couldn't have paid"); *but for*; *given (that), assuming (that), in the event of/that, on the assumption that, in that case*; ellipsis inside conditional clauses ("if (it is) possible").
  Ex: "Pay by Friday; otherwise a late fee applies." / "Few patients, if any, reported side effects." / "Given the shortage of nurses, the ward may close." Errors: *otherwise* used as *or*; *in case of* for *in the case of*; *if any* misplaced; *given that* + noun. Plan (14): gap 5 · mc 4 · transform 3 · error-spot 2.
- **L4 Modality in academic, legal and business register.** Rule: in formal texts modals carry fixed meanings — *shall* (obligation in contracts), *must* (requirement), *may* (permission or possibility), *will* (rule or promise), *should* (recommendation), *can* (general possibility) — and the reader is expected to know which. Cover: deontic (rules) vs epistemic (likelihood) readings; *shall* in contracts and statutes vs everyday *will*; *may* for permission in policies; *must* vs *should* in guidelines; *can* for general capability ("Errors can occur"); *would* in hypothetical and polite academic prose; idioms (*may well, might as well, may as well, cannot help but, would rather*); resolving ambiguous *may* (allowed or possible?).
  Ex: "The tenant shall keep the premises in good repair." / "Applicants may submit documents in English or French." / "The results may well change once the full data are analysed." Errors: *shall* for the plain future in essays; *can* for permission in policies (accepted informal; formal prefers *may*); *must* everywhere in recommendations; ambiguous *may*. Plan (14): classify 4 · mc 4 · gap 3 · error-spot 3.
- **L5 Subjunctive and formulaic conditionals.** Rule: formal English keeps a few old forms — *if I were to, were it not, be that as it may, come what may, so be it, lest* — and the mandative subjunctive after *insist, recommend, demand*.   Ex: "Were the hospital to lose its licence, 400 jobs would go." / "The committee demanded that the report be published." / "Be that as it may, the decision stands." Errors: "if I was to" (accepted informal); "demanded that the report is published" (*should be* accepted; indicative marked in formal items); "lest he will". Plan (14): gap 5 · transform 4 · mc 3 · error-spot 2.

### 7 · Foundations (A1) (I) — 5 lessons · 69 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 Adjectives describe nouns.** Rule: an adjective describes a noun; it goes before the noun ("a big house") or after *be* ("The house is big"), and it never changes for plural. Cover: position; no plural *-s* on adjectives; no gender agreement; common sets (size, colour, age, feelings, nationality); adjective + noun with *a/an* (sound rule); two adjectives with *and* after *be* ("small and cheap").

### 7 · Connecting (B1) — 6 lessons · 86 exercises · bank 120
- **L1 Adverbs that modify adjectives and other adverbs.** Rule: an adverb can make an adjective or another adverb stronger or weaker — extremely careful, quite slowly, really well. Cover: *too* + adjective/adverb (+ *to* + verb / *for* + person); *too much* (uncountable), *too many* (countable); *enough* after adjectives and adverbs, before nouns; *not… enough*; *too* ≠ *very*; *far too / much too*.

Plan (14): gap 5 · match 3 · mc 3 · error-spot 2 · say-it 1.
- **L6 Adverb position and sentence adverbs.** Rule: frequency and focus adverbs go in mid-position (before the main verb, after *be* or the first auxiliary); manner, place and time go at the end in that order; comment adverbs like *fortunately* go first with a comma. Cover: mid-position (always, also, just, still, already, probably, only, even); end-position order manner → place → time; front position for comment adverbs (*fortunately, unfortunately, hopefully* — *hopefully* as a sentence adverb accepted by M-W and AHD; some guides object → accept); *only* next to the word it limits in formal writing ("I ate only two"); *also* vs *too* vs *as well*; never between verb and object.
  Ex: "I have already sent the form." / "She probably works late on Fridays." / "Unfortunately, the bus was late again." Errors: "I speak always English"; "I like also"; "She has been never"; "I only ate two" (accepted informal); *also* at the end. Plan (14): order 5 · mc 4 · error-spot 3 · gap 2.
- Test: 15 of bank 120, Connecting mix. Remediation map: mc → L1-L6 · classify → L1 · gap → L1-L6 · order → L4, L6 · transform → L2-L4 · error-spot → L1-L6 · match → L5 · listen-choose → L1, L2 · dictation → L3. Certificate: **"Adjectives, Adverbs and Comparison, Connecting level"**.

### 7 · Expanding (B2) — 6 lessons · 86 exercises · bank 120
- **L1 Precise adjectives and register.** Rule: replace vague adjectives (good, bad, big, nice) with precise ones (effective, harmful, substantial, considerate) and choose intensifiers that fit the register (*very/really* informal; *highly, considerably, markedly, significantly* academic). Cover: the precision ladder; evaluative vs descriptive adjectives; collocation (*highly effective, deeply concerned, bitterly cold, heavily regulated*); no stacked intensifiers ("very extremely"); *quite/rather/fairly* nuance; workplace register (*adequate, satisfactory, outstanding*); VA24 8.LU.1.C "specific adjectives and adverbs".
  Ex: "The new schedule proved highly effective; overtime fell markedly." / "Patients were deeply concerned about the delay." / "The building is in a satisfactory but not good condition." Errors: *very* + everything (universal); translated collocations ("strong rain", "big price" — Spanish, Arabic, Chinese); *so* as an academic intensifier; *a lot* as an adverb in formal writing. Plan (14): mc 4 · match 3 · gap 3 · error-spot 2 · transform 2.
- **L2 Gradable and absolute adjectives.** Rule: gradable adjectives take *very/extremely* and comparatives; non-gradable ones (freezing, impossible, unique, dead, perfect, pregnant) take *absolutely/completely* and, in careful writing, are not compared. Cover: gradable vs limit adjectives; intensifier pairing (*absolutely exhausted, completely impossible, almost unique, nearly perfect*); *more perfect / most unique* — Merriam-Webster: attested and common; traditional guides object → error in formal items, accepted in informal; *almost/nearly/virtually* with absolutes are fine; *unique* (one of a kind) vs *unusual*.
  Ex: "The view from the summit was absolutely breathtaking." / "This manuscript is unique; the copy is nearly identical." / "The plan is impossible, not very impossible." Errors: "very unique", "most perfect", "very dead" (universal, native too); "absolutely big"; "quite unique" (BrE "completely"; accepted). Plan (14): classify 4 · mc 4 · error-spot 3 · gap 3.
- **L3 Adjective order and coordinate adjectives.** Rule: put adjectives in this order before a noun — opinion, size, physical quality, shape, age, colour, origin, material, purpose — and use a comma only between two adjectives of the same kind. Cover: Cambridge's ten-slot order (opinion, size, physical quality, shape, age, colour, origin, material, type, purpose) vs the eight-slot OSASCOMP taught in US classrooms (opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose) — they differ on shape/age and on "physical quality" → teach OSASCOMP, accept any order either list allows; coordinate adjectives (comma or *and*: "a long, boring meeting") vs cumulative (no comma: "a small wooden box"); the *and*-test and reverse-test (Purdue); rarely more than three adjectives.
  Ex: "She wore an elegant long black silk dress." / "They restored a beautiful old stone farmhouse." / "It was a cold, wet, miserable night." Errors: order transfer ("a black long dress" — Chinese, Arabic, Russian; Romance speakers put adjectives after the noun); commas between cumulative adjectives ("a small, wooden box"); *and* between size and colour. Plan (15): order 6 · mc 3 · error-spot 3 · gap 2 · classify 1.
- **L4 Adjective or adverb after linking verbs; *good/well, bad/badly*.** Rule: after linking verbs (be, seem, feel, look, smell, taste, sound, become) use an adjective; after action verbs use an adverb — feel bad but play badly; the soup smells good but she sings well. Cover: linking vs action verbs (Purdue "Adjective or adverb"); *good* (adjective) vs *well* (adverb; adjective = healthy); *I'm good* vs *I'm well* — M-W: both fine; *feel bad* (not *badly*); *look good/well*; *smell bad/badly* meaning change; *real good* (non-standard) vs *really good*; *-ly* adjectives after linking verbs ("seems friendly").
  Ex: "The bread smells wonderful, and it tastes even better." / "I felt bad about missing the meeting." / "She plays the violin well, and she plays it really beautifully." Errors: "I feel badly" (hypercorrection, native and ESL); "it tastes well"; "good in maths" (preposition); "he speaks very good"; "real good". Plan (14): mc 5 · gap 4 · error-spot 3 · classify 2.
- **L5 Stance and degree adverbs in academic writing; faulty comparisons.** Rule: use stance adverbs (arguably, presumably, notably, relatively, approximately) to show your position and precision, and make sure you compare like with like. Cover: stance adverbs and placement (sentence-initial with comma; mid-position); hedging adverbs (Section 6 Expanding L4); no *very/really/so* in academic prose; faulty comparison ("Ana's salary is higher than Luis" → "than Luis's"); *than* vs *then*; *than I / than me* (Section 3); *more so than*; *as opposed to*; *relative to*; double comparatives and "the most unique" as errors.
  Ex: "Night-shift nurses reported significantly higher stress than day-shift nurses." / "Arguably, the 1918 pandemic changed public health more than any war." / "The cost of the new drug is relatively low compared with older treatments." Errors: "higher than the day shift" (salary vs shift); "very significantly"; "more higher"; *then* for *than*; *compared to* vs *with* (accept both). Plan (14): error-spot 5 · transform 4 · mc 3 · gap 2.
- **L6 Participial and compound adjectives.** Rule: two or more words that describe a noun together are hyphenated before the noun ("a well-known inventor", "a state-of-the-art clinic") but not after it ("the inventor is well known"). Cover: compound modifiers hyphenated before the noun (Chicago, APA); no hyphen after *-ly* adverbs ("a highly skilled nurse"); *-ed* compounds ("a five-year-old child", "a broken-down truck"); *-ing* compounds ("an award-winning design"); numbers ("a 20-minute walk", "a two-thirds majority"); *much/well/little* + participle ("much appreciated", "well paid" vs "very tired"); suspended hyphens ("full- and part-time staff").
  Ex: "The award-winning nurse gave a well-prepared talk." / "The clinic is state of the art." / "We hired a highly skilled electrician for a two-day job." Errors: missing or extra hyphens ("well known inventor", "highly-skilled"); "a five-years-old"; "very appreciated" (accepted informal); "the most well-known" (accepted; *best-known* preferred). Plan (15): gap 5 · error-spot 4 · mc 3 · transform 3.
- Test: 15 of bank 120, Expanding mix. Remediation map: mc → L1-L6 · classify → L2-L4 · gap → L1-L6 · order → L3 · transform → L1, L5, L6 · error-spot → L1-L6 · match → L1 · listen-choose → L4. Certificate: **"Adjectives, Adverbs and Comparison, Expanding level"**.

### 7 · Mastery (C1) — 5 lessons · 71 exercises · bank 110
- **L1 Shades of meaning and connotation.** Rule: near-synonyms carry different attitudes and collocate with different words — thrifty, frugal and stingy all mean "careful with money" but only one is a compliment — so choose by connotation and by the words that normally go together. Cover: denotation vs connotation (slim/thin/skinny; confident/arrogant; curious/nosy; childlike/childish); collocation restrictions (*heavy rain, strong coffee, high price, deep concern, broad agreement*); register (*kids/children, guy/man, big/considerable*); intensifier-adjective collocations (*bitterly disappointed, fully aware, highly unlikely*); dictionary and collocation checks (Oxford, Cambridge).
  Ex: "The council took a cautious, not a timid, approach." / "The company faces strong competition and heavy losses." / "She was bitterly disappointed but fully aware of the risks." Errors: translated collocations ("powerful rain", "expensive price" — Spanish, Arabic, Chinese, Russian); register clashes in reports ("a huge problem" for "a serious problem"); thesaurus over-substitution. Plan (14): match 4 · mc 4 · gap 3 · error-spot 2 · classify 1.
- **L2 Figurative language under control.** Rule: metaphor, simile, personification and idiom can sharpen writing, but use them sparingly, keep the images consistent, and leave them out of scientific and legal prose. Cover: metaphor ("the market crashed"), simile ("as clear as day"), personification ("the city never sleeps"), hyperbole, idioms with adjectives and adverbs (*a bitter dispute, a heated debate, a sweeping change*); dead metaphors acceptable in business and academic prose ("a rise in prices"); mixed metaphors; genre limits (reports, contracts, science); WIDA-LC L6 "figurative language".
  Ex: "Inflation ate into savings, and the poorest families felt it first." / "The bridge, a steel ribbon across the gorge, opened in 1937." / "The company weathered the storm but lost half its crew." Errors: idioms translated literally (universal); mixed metaphors; figurative language in method sections; clichés ("at the end of the day"). Plan (14): classify 4 · mc 3 · transform 4 · error-spot 3.
- **L3 Adverbs for rhetorical effect and stance.** Rule: sentence adverbs (admittedly, ironically, frankly), focusing adverbs (even, only, particularly) and understatement (not insignificant) shape how the reader takes a claim — use them deliberately and place them next to what they modify. Cover: disjuncts (*admittedly, frankly, ironically, regrettably*) with a comma; focusing adverbs (*even, only, just, particularly, especially, solely*) and placement; *literally* as an intensifier (M-W records it; avoid in formal writing); *hopefully* (accepted); litotes (*not unusual, not insignificant*); irony and emphasis; punctuation of fronted adverbs; register (*frankly* in speech vs reports).
  Ex: "Admittedly, the first trial was small." / "Even the most experienced surgeons make mistakes." / "The delay was not insignificant: it cost the firm a contract." Errors: *literally* for emphasis in essays; *even* misplaced; *only* ambiguity ("only nurses can apply / nurses can only apply"); "frankly speaking" over-use (Chinese, Russian). Plan (14): mc 4 · order 3 · transform 3 · error-spot 3 · gap 1.
- **L4 Precise comparison and gradation.** Rule: quantify comparisons — twice as likely, a 20% higher rate, more than double, second only to — and use *the elder / the latter / further* correctly. Cover: *twice / three times as… as*, *x% higher/lower than*, *more than double*, *-fold*, *comparable to, on a par with, second only to, by a wide margin*; *the better of the two* (comparative for two, superlative for three or more — traditional; M-W: superlative for two is common) → teach the comparative, accept the superlative in informal items; *elder/eldest* (family, before the noun) vs *older*; *further* (figurative, additional) vs *farther* (distance) — M-W: overlap accepted → accept both for distance, *further* only for "additional"; *latter/last, former/first*.
  Ex: "Night workers are twice as likely to report poor sleep." / "Of the two clinics, the older one is the better equipped." / "The eldest son took over the farm; the younger children moved to the city." Errors: "twice more likely than" (Romance); "the best of the two" (accepted informal); "farther information"; "the most oldest". Plan (15): gap 5 · transform 4 · mc 3 · error-spot 3.
- **L5 Word-class flexibility: adjective and adverb forms in academic prose.** Rule: many words change class with a suffix (economy → economic/economical → economically), and some adverbs change meaning with *-ly* (hard/hardly, late/lately, high/highly) — check the form and the meaning, not just the ending. Cover: adjective suffixes (*-al, -ic, -ical, -ive, -ous, -able, -ful, -less*) and pairs with different meanings (*economic/economical, historic/historical, classic/classical, sensible/sensitive*); adverb pairs (*hard/hardly, late/lately, high/highly, most/mostly, near/nearly, short/shortly, free/freely*); *-ly* adjectives (*friendly, likely, costly, timely, daily*) and their adverbial phrases ("in a timely manner"); *likely* as an adverb ("will likely" AmE; BrE prefers "is likely to") → accept both; words that are both (*fast, early, daily, weekly, straight*).
  Ex: "The policy was economical but not economic in its effects." / "The results will likely change once the full data are in." / "She hardly ever works late, though lately she has." Errors: "economical growth", "historical moment" for *historic*; *hardly* for *hard*; *friendly* as an adverb ("he spoke friendly"); "in a daily basis". Plan (14): gap 5 · match 3 · mc 3 · error-spot 3.
- Test: 15 of bank 110, Mastery mix. Remediation map: mc → L1-L5 · classify → L1, L2 · gap → L1, L3-L5 · order → L3 · transform → L2-L4 · error-spot → L1-L5 · match → L1, L5 · listen-choose → L3. Certificate: **"Adjectives, Adverbs and Comparison, Mastery level"**.
